# Chapter 7: A Woman Named Tamar

## The Return

The petitioners had gone home, but the household did not return with them.

Soldiers drilled in the lower court under Rufus's harder eye. Roman servants complained that local workers had carried bread to people who shamed the prefect. Two kitchen hands failed to appear on the morning after the images left Jerusalem. Philotas marked them absent and asked whether Tamar's leave should be entered the same way.

"Paid leave," Claudia said.

"She left before her duty ended."

"With my permission."

"The others will ask for equal treatment."

"Then we will hear why they were absent."

Philotas placed the tablet before her. "A household cannot inquire into every grief."

"It can inquire before it withholds wages."

The distinction would require time, which meant Marcus's time and Philotas's resentment. Claudia signed the entry.

Tamar returned near midday with Yaakov beside her. His right arm hung in a sling. He was thinner than the man Claudia remembered from the market, his face burned dark from five days in the open. He stopped at the service threshold and refused to enter farther.

"The shoulder is not broken," Tamar said. "A physician at the harbor set it. Yaakov cannot lift a jar or pull a cart for some weeks."

"Who paid the physician?"

"His wife borrowed from Rivkah."

"Rivkah is ill."

"She still has money."

Tamar's answer closed the subject. Claudia saw that charity offered too quickly could expose the borrowing within a family that had not invited Roman inspection.

Yaakov thanked her for allowing the sellers through the household gate. His gratitude was formal. When Claudia asked what happened in the stadium, he said only that the prefect removed the images.

"Did the soldiers strike anyone there?"

"No."

"Were all the petitioners prepared to die?"

His eyes moved to Tamar and back. "I cannot answer for all. One man beside me tried to leave. Another held him until the passage opened. Some knelt because the elders knelt. Some were ready before we entered. I was afraid."

"Yet you stayed."

"Yes."

Claudia had wanted a clean fact to take from the encounter. Yaakov gave her a crowded one: fear, pressure, conviction, and obedience could occupy the same stone tier.

She offered to have the palace physician examine the shoulder. Yaakov refused.

"The prefect's soldiers caused the injury," Tamar said after he left. "He will not enter the prefect's house to have it measured."

"The shield struck him outside the service road. Pontius did not order that blow."

"The shield belonged to Pontius."

Claudia felt the Roman defense rise and recognized it because she had heard its shape from her husband. "What would help?"

"Do not ask me while Yaakov is still at the gate. Ask his wife."

It was a correction and a condition. Tamar would report what she knew. She would not turn her family into Claudia's household problem for the convenience of being helped.

## What the House Owed

Rivkah died nine days later.

Tamar did not come to work. Philotas brought the news with the wage tablet already open and a replacement assigned to the eastern court.

"How many days did you enter?" Claudia asked.

"Seven absent. Unpaid unless you order otherwise."

"What does the household owe her?"

Philotas looked genuinely puzzled. "Her wages for days worked."

"She has served since our arrival. She left once under my order, and now the woman who raised her has died. What provision did Gratus's household make?"

"Roman staff have burial clubs. Local hires arrange their own customs. If we begin paying kinship losses, every cousin in Caesarea will become a parent."

The objection was crude, though the administrative problem beneath it was real. Household records named workers and wages. They did not name dependents, burial obligations, or injuries that kept a family from earning. Any payment Claudia made without a rule would be a favor that disappeared with the next steward.

"Hold Tamar's place for seven days," she said. "Pay her ordinary wage. No replacement is to be made permanent."

"The kitchen rota will require extra watches."

"Pay those as well."

"From which account?"

"Mine."

Philotas wrote slowly enough to display disapproval.

Claudia went to the mourning house on the third day with Marcus and no palace guard. Roman decorum objected to the visit. Household prudence objected more strongly after the standards crisis. She went before the objection could harden into Pontius's order.

The house stood two streets from the harbor. Neighbors filled the small courtyard and two rooms beyond it. Tamar sat low among them with a tear at the collar of her tunic. When she saw Claudia, gratitude did not appear. Alarm did.

"You should not have come, Domina."

"I held your place and wages for seven days."

"That could have been told at the kitchen."

Claudia heard the mistake. She had entered mourning carrying an answer before asking what was wanted.

"May I sit?"

Tamar pointed to an empty place near the wall.

No one explained the torn garments. No one arranged the mourners for her understanding. A neighbor set bread and lentils on a cloth. The loaves were round, though Claudia did not ask why. Rivkah's absence occupied the room without needing an interpreter.

Marcus remained by the doorway. A man asked whether he was Claudia's freedman. Marcus answered, "I belong to the prefect's household." The man's face changed. Free Tamar could refuse the palace gate. Marcus had followed Claudia into a house where her rank protected her and marked him.

After a time, Tamar's uncle came from the inner room with a folded account of burial expenses. He did not offer it to Claudia. He asked Tamar whether the palace would demand proof of death before paying her wages.

"No," Claudia said. "Your household owes no proof to mine."

Marcus shifted at the doorway. Later he would tell her that a record without a witness could be challenged by Philotas, leaving Tamar vulnerable to repayment.

Tamar answered her uncle in Aramaic. Claudia understood only Rivkah's name and the word for house.

"I told him the wage is yours to grant," Tamar said. "That does not make it his to ask from you."

Claudia stayed until the light changed. She learned little she could repeat. Grief had reduced speech to practical fragments: who would sleep in the outer room, which neighbor had brought oil, whether Yaakov could carry a stool with his good arm.

Before Claudia left, Yaakov's wife, Miriam, stopped her in the passage.

"He cannot work the cart," she said in careful Greek. "He will borrow again before he asks Rome for a coin."

"What work does he do?"

"He carries jars and stone from the harbor."

"Would he accept wages for work he can do with one arm?"

Miriam considered. "He might accept work from Mattan. He will not accept the prefect's gift."

That was an answer Claudia could use without asking Tamar to betray him.

## The Account

Back at the palace, Marcus placed the wage tablet beside the service-gate receipts.

"Philotas will need a reason for seven paid days," he said. "If the reason is only your order, the next steward may call it an advance and recover it."

"Then how was your burial club recorded in Rome?"

"The club belonged to enslaved staff in your guardian's house. A coin was withheld each month. When a named member of the household died, the steward released a fixed sum. Some men paid for years and were sold before they used it."

Marcus gave the machinery without praising it.

"Could this household record something smaller for free workers?"

"It could record wages held during an approved absence. Burial money would require names of kin. Some workers will not want the prefect's office keeping those names."

"The record stays in the household account."

"Household records can still be read by a prefect."

Claudia looked at him. "And by you."

"Yes, Domina."

The danger belonged to the method, not to the reader's virtue.

They drafted a narrow rule. A free household worker could name one dependent or the person on whom the worker depended. After a death, serious illness, or injury, Claudia could authorize up to seven days of held wages and a fixed payment for food, burial, or physician's fees. The central tablet would record only the worker, sum, and category. A separate folded list of names would remain under Claudia's household seal. Marcus would record the payments. Philotas would witness the coin leaving the chest. Neither man could add a beneficiary.

"And if the injury came from Roman soldiers?" Marcus asked.

"The category says injury."

"That conceals the cause."

"It protects Yaakov's name."

"It also protects the prefect."

Claudia disliked the truth enough to leave the line unfinished.

She took the proposal to Pontius that evening. He was reading Rufus's report on morale after the stadium.

"Seven days of wages for every cousin who dies?" he asked.

"One named obligation for each free worker. Fixed limits. Household funds."

"Paid by you."

"At first."

"Then the practice will become expected, and expectation becomes cost."

"So does replacing trained workers because a burial leaves them hungry."

He turned the tablet over. "You want to pay a man who petitioned against me."

"I want to hire him for work he can do while his shoulder heals."

"That is a gift wearing a wage."

"Then give him work worth the coin."

Pontius's expression closed. "He does not enter the records rooms, the armory court, or any prefectural office."

"He carries purchase tablets between Mattan's sellers and the outer store. One arm is enough. He reports to Mattan, not Marcus."

"For how long?"

"Thirty days, reviewed each week."

"And when every petitioner asks for household work?"

"They will be refused unless the household needs them."

Pontius approved the trial because the expense came from Claudia's allowance and because keeping injured men employed was cheaper than letting Roman actions become public beggary. He required Philotas to send him a monthly total. The concession gave Claudia a practice and gave Pontius sight of its cost.

The next morning the Roman kitchen staff objected. Two extra watches during Tamar's leave had been paid, yet Sulpicia argued that Claudia favored local grief while Roman servants worked through their own losses.

Claudia extended the held-wage rule to every free worker in the household. Enslaved staff remained under a separate allowance controlled by the owner. Marcus pointed out the inequality without needing to name it.

"Can you be entered?" Claudia asked.

"No, Domina. I have no wage to hold."

She could direct money for his food or burial through household ownership. She could not make his position resemble Tamar's by changing a heading.

Claudia increased the enslaved staff burial allotment from her own account and forbade Philotas to deduct it later from rations. The act cost more than she had planned and still left control in Roman hands.

## Conditions

Tamar returned after seven days. Her place remained open. Her wages had been marked held rather than advanced. She read the new rule while Marcus explained which names would appear on which tablet.

"You made this because Rivkah died?" she asked.

"Because Philotas had no answer when I asked what the house owed."

"Rivkah did not work here."

"You do."

Tamar set down the tablet. "And now your house will hold the names of our families."

"Only if a worker chooses to give one."

"A choice offered by the prefect's wife is still heard with the prefect behind it."

Claudia had written voluntary beside the line and believed the word sufficient. Tamar showed her why it was not.

"What would make the refusal real?"

"Let a worker take the seven days without naming the dead. Require a name only for money paid outside the household, and let that name be held by someone who can refuse you."

"Who?"

"Mattan, perhaps. He knows the free workers. He is free himself."

Marcus did not react, though the change removed part of the list from his hands.

Claudia revised the rule. Absence and held wages required two household witnesses, with no kin named. Outside payment required a sealed note from Mattan confirming that the recipient had agreed to receive it. The central account kept the sum and purpose. Mattan kept the name. Marcus kept the Roman payment trail. The two records could be joined only if both men cooperated.

"It may invite fraud," Claudia said.

"Yes," Tamar answered. "Mattan may favor his cousins. A worker may lie. A Roman steward may still ask him for the list."

"You recommend it."

"I say it exposes fewer people. That is not the same thing."

Conditional trust sounded like that: an offered use followed by the description of its danger.

Mattan found work for Yaakov carrying tablets and checking deliveries at the outer store. Yaakov accepted because the wage matched the task and because Mattan, not Claudia, made the offer. His shoulder kept him from lifting jars. His knowledge of the harbor sellers let him catch a delivery entered twice in the first week.

Marcus recorded the correction. He did not question Yaakov about the stadium. Their work touched at the account and stopped there.

The first test of the new boundary came sooner than Claudia expected. A Roman store clerk asked Mattan for the names behind three outside payments. He said the prefectural secretary required them to prevent fraud. Mattan refused and sent the request to Claudia under his own seal.

The prefectural secretary denied giving the instruction. Philotas recognized the clerk's hand from a complaint by veterans who believed the service gate had favored the petitioners. The man had wanted to learn which household workers had relatives in the crowd.

Pontius proposed dismissal for using the prefectural office falsely. Claudia stopped short of that. Dismissal could drive the clerk to sell the names he already guessed, and she had learned what a Roman accusation could do beyond the palace.

She removed him from local accounts, docked the fee he had charged for copying the request, and assigned him to the western stores under Philotas's direct witness. The punishment angered Roman staff who believed she had protected disloyal workers. It angered Tamar for a different reason.

"He tried to find our families and keeps his place," Tamar said.

"In a post where he cannot read your payments."

"He still knows why he was moved. So does every servant who watched."

Claudia had reduced his access and preserved the household's labor. She had also announced that the divided record contained something worth seeking.

"Would you have me dismiss him?"

"I would have you know that protecting the list once will not make it safe."

Claudia ordered Mattan to keep no standing list. After each payment, he would return the recipient's sealed consent or destroy it in the recipient's presence. Marcus would retain only the sum, date, and purpose. The change weakened later proof if a payment was disputed. It also left fewer names for a curious clerk to find.

The relief payment for Rivkah's burial went to Tamar after she asked for it, three days after returning. It covered oil and part of the bread supplied by neighbors. She took the coins without gratitude performed for the household.

"This does not settle what happened with the standards," she said.

"I did not think it did."

"Yaakov believes the prefect yielded because he feared a complaint to Rome. My uncle believes God turned his hand. The elder beside them says discipline saved their lives. They argue each time they meet."

"Which do you believe?"

"That they came home. I know the rest only in pieces."

Claudia had once wanted Tamar to give her the province's hidden meaning. The answer before her was smaller and more credible. Tamar knew a family, a market, a mourning house, and what people said after returning. Marcus knew sums, absences, seals, and the order in which papers moved. Together their accounts could correct an official version. Neither could replace it or the other.

At the end of the month, Philotas brought Claudia the first total: held wages for three workers, burial food for Tamar, physician's fees for a stableman's child, and thirty days of light work for Yaakov. The sum had consumed nearly half her quarterly household allowance.

Pontius initialed the total without comment. Rufus's military issues no longer passed through Marcus's schedule. Claudia had gained one channel and lost sight of another.

She asked Marcus to begin a second monthly line for injuries reported by household workers or their named dependents, whether the house paid anything or not. He would record the place, the kind of injury, who witnessed it, and whether a physician had been called. Names would remain with Mattan unless a worker chose to place one under Claudia's seal.

"An account without names may be challenged," Marcus said.

"Then mark who holds the name."

"If many injuries occur at once, the total itself will draw questions."

"It should."

That answer carried its own danger. A later prefectural inquiry might use the pattern to find people the divided record tried to protect. Claudia required her seal before any total left the household office. The practice could support relief after another public injury. It could also become a guide to the injured if she lost control of it.

She sealed the folded payment notes and gave Mattan his separate list. From then on, anyone who accepted the household's help would create a trace, even divided between hands. The trace might later protect a wage, prove an injury, or draw official attention to a family.

Tamar watched her close the chest.

"If you keep the account," she said, "you are responsible when someone uses it."

Claudia placed the key among her own tablets. The practice had begun with seven days of grief and one injured shoulder. It would continue only if she guarded the people inside it from the household that paid.
