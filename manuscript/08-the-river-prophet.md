# Chapter 8: The River Prophet

*POV: Claudia*

## News Carried Overland

Two years had worn the household into a shape Claudia no longer thought of as foreign. She knew which market stalls sold fish honestly weighed, knew the trumpet calls that divided the garrison watch, and knew enough of Tamar's calendar to expect the kitchen quiet on the seventh day. Caesarea had become a place whose disagreements reached her by several doors.

Philotas brought the first word of the preacher with the same flat competence he brought to grain prices and road repairs. Claudia almost let it pass.

"There is a man at the Jordan drawing crowds, Domina. Out past Jericho, where the river narrows enough to wade. The garrison at the border post sends word because the numbers have grown large enough to concern them, though nothing has happened beyond the numbers themselves."

"Drawing crowds to do what?"

"To be washed, Domina. Some say it concerns forgiveness. The officer wrote only that they enter the river one at a time." Philotas delivered this with the faint discomfort of a man reporting something he did not personally credit. "He is called John. Soldiers on the bank describe skins rather than wool."

The skins could wait. Claudia needed the numbers. Rome had seen prophets before, wandering men with a handful of followers and a grievance against the way the world was arranged. None of them had troubled a border garrison enough to reach a prefect's steward. "How large a crowd."

"Large enough that the centurion posted there asked whether he should disperse it. The report gives no count. Pontius said not yet."

Claudia asked Marcus to bring the tablet and the border notice that preceded it. Military issues no longer passed through his hands, but incoming notices still entered the household schedule when Pontius expected a visitor or reply.

The earlier notice described a roadside preacher and people entering the Jordan. The later one described an assembly impeding grazing near the ford. Neither named a seized weapon, unpaid tax, injured person, or refused order. The claim that John condemned soldiers came through an unnamed listener.

The notices did not establish when John began preaching, how often he moved along the river, or whether the two officers had observed the same place. The first Roman record marked the administration's awareness, not the beginning of the movement.

Marcus marked the differences on a household copy: date, sender, place, reported act, and what the writer had only heard. Beside crowd size he wrote *unfixed*.

"Keep this with petitions and unusual gatherings," Claudia said. "Link it by place."

"The prefectural secretary may ask why we copied a military notice."

"Then show him the household schedule. Philotas brought it because Pontius may receive the border officer."

The authority was narrow and visible. Claudia preferred that to pretending the copy had appeared by accident.

## What the Water Was Doing

Tamar had already heard of him. Her news of the province arrived faster than any dispatch Philotas carried, and more candidly.

"He is not the first to preach at the river," Tamar said, working oil into a cloth for the lamps. "There have been others, over the years. Most gather a season's following and are forgotten by the next. This one is different."

"Different how?"

"My uncle heard that he sends people home," Tamar said. "A seller from Jericho says some remain near the ford. They may have heard him on different days."

"And the report that he speaks against soldiers and collectors?"

"Yaakov heard that he tells them to take only what they are owed. He heard it from a carter who did not go into the water."

"What do you know yourself?"

"That people in the market argue over whether to go. Two workers asked for leave. Neither could tell me how many were there."

Claudia thought of the mourning house, the round loaves, the plain report of Rivkah's death given without performance. "You make it sound like the same hope you spoke of after your aunt died."

"Perhaps. I have not heard him."

"And the water. Why the water."

"Ask Simon when he returns. He went with his uncle."

The answer corrected Claudia's question. Tamar could tell her how news moved through the market. She could not supply the preacher's purpose because Claudia wanted one.

## Leave for the Ford

The two workers who asked for leave were brothers employed in the outer store. Philotas refused them. The household held wages for burial, illness, and injury under Claudia's rule. A journey to hear a preacher met none of those conditions.

"They may go without wages," he said. "If they return when the rota requires them."

The older brother accepted. The younger withdrew his request after a Roman clerk warned that anyone visiting the ford might be named in the border officer's next inquiry.

Claudia summoned the clerk. He insisted he had only repeated what soldiers said in the market.

"Which soldiers?"

"Men from the eastern road."

"Names."

He had none. The warning might be loose barracks talk, a threat invented to discourage absence, or a real question moving ahead of written orders. Claudia could prove only that it had changed one worker's choice.

She upheld Philotas's unpaid-leave decision. Paying people to attend would entangle the household in a gathering Pontius had not classified. She also ordered that no absence entry name John, the Jordan, or a religious purpose unless the worker chose to state it. The rota would show personal leave and the lost wage.

Philotas objected. "Then we cannot know whether three men use one excuse in turn."

"Record whether the shift was covered and whether the worker returned. The household needs those facts."

"The prefect may need the destination."

"Then the prefect may ask for it under his own authority."

The distinction protected the workers from a clerk's curiosity and made Claudia responsible if the omission later appeared deliberate. Marcus entered the order under her seal.

The older brother returned six days later, two days beyond his leave. Philotas wanted dismissal. He had left the store short-handed during a grain delivery, and a Roman porter had been paid for two extra watches.

The man said flooding at a crossing delayed him. Tamar had heard the same from a spice seller whose cart arrived late. A road notice reported no flood, though its courier had used the northern route.

Claudia could not establish the truth. She deducted the uncovered days, charged the extra porter against the worker's next two weeks, and retained him under a thirty-day warning. Tamar thought the deduction harsh. Philotas thought retention indulgent.

"Did he hear John?" Claudia asked Tamar afterward.

"He says he did."

"What did he bring back?"

"That the preacher condemned Herod's marriage. His brother says that story came from a traveler before he left."

The report introduced the tetrarch into the matter and arrived through a man who needed Claudia to believe his journey had been worth the disruption. She recorded both the claim and the reason for caution.

Marcus asked whether the worker should be named.

"He agreed to speak about the delay because his employment was at risk," Claudia said. "That is not agreement to enter a political report. Mark the outer store and the date."

"An unnamed late worker will carry little weight."

"Then it should carry little weight."

Claudia had protected a source by making the information weaker. She had also imposed a wage cost on the same man. Household power did not become innocent because she used it carefully.

## Those Who Went and Came Back

The cook's boy went to the Jordan the following month. Simon was a quiet child Claudia had exchanged perhaps a dozen words with in two years. He walked the distance with his uncle over four days and came back with a sunburn across his neck and a manner Tamar noticed before Claudia did.

"He is different," Tamar said, watching the boy carry water across the courtyard with none of his usual slouching haste. "Ask him what he saw. It will come plainer than from soldiers counting heads at a distance."

Claudia found him at the woodpile the next morning. He answered without the shyness she expected, as though he had been waiting for someone to ask.

"I heard him speak of trees cut down for bearing nothing," Simon said. "My uncle says he spoke against collectors. I remember him answering one, though I could not hear every word."

"And the washing itself. What was it like."

The boy turned a stick of kindling in his hands. "Cold. He asked me something first. I could not hear well with the people behind us, and my uncle remembers different words."

"What do you remember?"

"That I should leave one thing behind and do differently when I went home. That may be what I expected him to say."

"Did he say anything of a king. Of when the world would be set right."

"I heard people repeat that another would come. One man said king. My uncle says the preacher never used that word."

"Which man?"

"I do not know his name."

Claudia could not separate the preacher's words from Simon's expectation by listening harder. She would have to preserve the uncertainty.

## The Tetrarch's Border

She raised it with Pontius that evening in the study, where the lamp burned later now that the aqueduct survey took so many of his hours.

"It is not my border to police," he said, without looking up from the wax tablet he was correcting. "The Jordan crossing where the man preaches falls inside Herod Antipas's tetrarchy. Galilee and Perea. My post there watches the river because Rome likes a watcher on every river. The man himself is no concern of mine."

"Antipas." Claudia turned the name over. She had heard it before, folded into other names in other reports, never attached to anything worth remembering. "You have not spoken of him."

"There is little to say that flatters him. Herod's son, one of several, given Galilee and Perea when the old king's territory was divided among his heirs, and given none of the father's competence with it. He builds cities and names them for the Emperor and for himself in the same breath." Pontius set the stylus down, which he did only when a thing had taken more attention than he meant to give it. "A tetrarch keeps his title by convincing Rome he keeps his corner quiet. A man who can call up a crowd that size without lifting a finger makes an uncomfortable neighbor for him."

"Do you think he means harm. The preacher."

"I think he means what your household tells you he means." He said it without irony. It was as close as Pontius came to admitting that what moved through Tamar's kitchen reached him too, by way of his wife rather than any report his own men filed. "I have no complaint against a man who tells soldiers to take only their wages and collectors to take only what is owed. My concern is narrower. A crowd that size has gathered on a river Antipas cannot fully police. What he does about it will not happen on my side of the border."

He went back to the tablet, and Claudia understood the audience was closing. Something in it stayed with her.

Her husband trusted ledgers over rumor in every other matter she had watched him weigh, and he had not once suggested the preacher's numbers were exaggerated or that the crowds would thin with the season. That was how he dismissed most unrest reaching him secondhand.

He had measured this one and then set it across a border. Claudia could not decide whether that was judgment or convenience.

The next morning a letter arrived from Antipas's household secretary. It thanked Pontius for leaving order at the ford to the tetrarch's officers and stated that the gathering remained religious in character. A second paragraph requested notice if subjects of Judaea crossed the boundary in organized numbers.

The letter called the movement harmless and asked Rome to watch who joined it.

Claudia placed it beside the border reports, Tamar's market account, and the account Simon had given at the woodpile. Marcus ruled five headings on a fresh tablet: source, place, witnessed, heard, and interest.

The centurion had witnessed obstruction near a ford and had reason to make numbers urgent. Tamar had witnessed household talk and market disagreement. Simon had witnessed the preacher and the washing, though memory and other voices had entered his account. Antipas's secretary had witnessed none of it in the letter and had reason to keep responsibility inside his court while sharing risk with Pontius.

Claudia called Simon back and read him the notes attributed to him. He corrected two. He had heard the image of the axe himself, but the words about grain and chaff came from his uncle afterward. The unnamed man who said *king* had been repeating another traveler.

"Will this go to the prefect?" Simon asked.

"The comparison will. Your name will not unless you agree or unless I believe someone faces immediate harm."

"Then I do not agree."

Claudia told Marcus to identify him as *household worker returned from the ford*. The protection weakened the report and kept a kitchen boy from becoming a named Roman source by accident.

No version established an army, tax refusal, or call to violence. None settled what the washing meant. Royal language rested on a chain no one could recover.

Claudia took the comparison to Pontius without Simon's name.

"No present offense in Judaea," Pontius said. "No Roman action."

"Keep the accounts linked."

"For a preacher outside my jurisdiction?"

"For the next report. If Antipas changes the word he uses for the gathering, we should know what changed before the word did."

Pontius allowed the household copy to remain outside the prefectural file. He required Claudia to bring him any report of weapons, refused taxes, blocked roads, or a named claim to kingship. The arrangement protected Simon and made Claudia responsible for deciding when uncertainty crossed that threshold.

Marcus tied the tablets with the location slip visible. The matter stayed unresolved and stayed open, kept in a form Claudia could use when the next distant figure arrived through contradictory reports.
