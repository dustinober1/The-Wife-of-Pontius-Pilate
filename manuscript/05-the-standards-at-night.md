# Chapter 5: The Standards at Night

*POV: Claudia*

## Three Reports

The Antonia report reached Caesarea before sunrise.

Claudia knew because Marcus crossed the eastern court carrying a sealed tablet to the prefectural secretary at an hour when the household lamps were still burning. He returned without it, but he had kept the courier schedule under his arm.

"From the fortress?" she asked.

"The Antonia commander, Domina. The gate officer's rider is due after midday. No message has come through the high priest's liaison."

Pontius had ordered three accounts. One had arrived, and the silence of another already said something.

Philotas brought the public news an hour later. The winter detachment had entered Jerusalem after dark with its standards covered. At dawn the images were raised from the fortress overlooking the Temple.

"The commander's report calls the transfer orderly," he said. "No resistance at the gate. No injury. No breach of the Temple courts."

"Does it say who saw the standards uncovered?"

"It says the garrison assumed its proper station."

The sentence had closed around the event before the city had found words for it.

Claudia found Pontius in the audience hall reading the same report. His breakfast stood untouched beside him.

"You raised them after entry."

"As ordered."

"The liaison has sent nothing."

"Because nothing requires an answer. The cohort entered an existing Roman post. The images remain outside the Temple."

He spoke with the calm of a man reviewing a completed movement. Claudia put the missing liaison message beside the commander's clean account and heard Hanan's warning again: the city would read the image as a loyalty test.

"You created the channel to prevent surprise," she said.

"I created it for questions that fall within its terms. This was a military rotation."

"Then why were the standards covered?"

"To avoid a street obstruction."

"That is why no resistance appears in the report. The city did not see the decision until the soldiers were behind fortress walls."

Pontius set the tablet down. "The order is complete, Claudia. I will not negotiate the Emperor's image out of a Roman garrison."

She left before the argument reduced itself to the same two sentences. Her immediate need was narrower. She wanted the other reports before Pontius sent his memorandum north to Syria with the commander's version standing alone.

Marcus showed her the courier order in the records passage. The Syrian packet was scheduled for the second hour after midday. The gate officer's rider should arrive one hour earlier, if the road had held. A notation in another hand showed that a messenger from Jerusalem had requested entry through the household court.

"Who moved him from the audience list?" Claudia asked.

"The prefectural secretary. He was described as a servant from the high priest's house without a formal seal."

"Where is he?"

"At the outer gate. He may still be there."

Claudia sent Marcus to tell Philotas that the messenger had household business with her. It was true only because she had made it true. Marcus could move the name between lists; he could not admit the man against a guard's order, and neither of them could give the message formal standing.

The messenger was a gray-bearded steward Claudia had seen beside Hanan during the delegation. He gave his name as Mattithiah and refused wine.

"The liaison was told after dawn," he said. "He went to the high priest's house. By then men were already gathering below the fortress."

"Why no sealed protest?"

"The seal keeper is in the Temple precinct. The streets between are full. I was sent from the household before the council agreed on words."

He could not know what the council would decide. He did know the order in which news had moved through one household. Claudia asked him to repeat only what he had witnessed. He had seen the images above the fortress. He had seen shopkeepers closing stalls near the western street. He had heard men argue over whether to go to the Antonia or carry a petition to Caesarea. He had not seen weapons. He would not promise there were none.

Claudia wrote four lines and made him mark them before Marcus carried a copy to the prefectural secretary: the liaison received no prior notice; the standards were visible after dawn; gatherings had formed; no armed action was witnessed by the messenger. She did not call it the liaison's report.

Pontius read the note before the Syrian packet closed. He ordered Mattithiah's statement attached beneath the commander's report and changed nothing else.

"An unsealed household witness," he said. "Useful as warning, insufficient as policy."

"Then wait for the gate officer."

"A courier schedule does not govern me."

"You ordered separate reports because one office could mistake silence for peace."

That reached him. He delayed the Syrian rider until midday.

He gave her one hour. Claudia had wanted more. He had first refused to give any.

## The Road from Jerusalem

The gate officer's account arrived late. A lame remount had cost the rider nearly two hours. The report confirmed no violence at entry and added what the commander had omitted: residents had begun following the soldiers before the column reached the Antonia, and by sunrise a delegation was demanding removal of the images.

The formal liaison protest came behind it. Hanan described the concealed approach as a breach of the prefect's own channel and asked for the standards to be removed before the dispute spread beyond Jerusalem.

Pontius received both after his Syrian memorandum had gone. He wrote a second dispatch stating that objections had arisen after an orderly transfer. Claudia watched the secretary place it in the next courier case. The order of arrival would remain visible in Syria: Roman completion first, local objection afterward.

By evening, riders from Jerusalem had carried a quicker account along the coast road. Tamar heard it from a cousin of Mattan's who sold oil near the south gate. The men gathering in Jerusalem meant to come to Caesarea, though no one agreed how many or when.

"Yaakov will come," Tamar said.

Claudia remembered the gray shawl Tamar's cousin wore on market days. "How do you know?"

"His wife sent to my aunt for bread that will keep on the road. He told her he would petition until the images were removed."

"Does he speak for the others?"

"He speaks for himself. He knows some men from his quarter. That is all."

The limit mattered. Mattithiah knew a priestly household. Tamar knew one cousin and the preparations around him. The gate officer knew what passed under his wall. None could name the whole city.

Claudia asked what Yaakov expected to find at Caesarea.

"A hearing, perhaps. Refusal, certainly. He thinks the prefect will yield when he sees they came unarmed."

"And if Pontius does not?"

Tamar's hands stopped over the folded linen. "Yaakov believes men beside him will stay. He may be wrong about some of them. He has been wrong before."

Claudia went to Pontius with the gate report, Hanan's sealed protest, and her own short account of Tamar's warning. She left Mattithiah's conjectures out and named Yaakov as a single household connection rather than a spokesman.

"They are organizing for the road," she said. "You can hear a delegation before a crowd forms here."

"I have already heard their delegation."

"You heard Hanan before you acted. Hear those living with the act."

Pontius denied an advance audience. He ordered the outer court prepared for petitioners and instructed the guard to admit only recognized elders through the main gate. The rest were to remain outside.

Claudia had altered his preparation, though his decision stood.

## The Petitioners

They arrived in separate groups over two days. Claudia saw no count she trusted. The guard recorded adult men who approached the gate, Philotas counted households requiring water, and Tamar recognized people who had joined along the road without ever appearing before a clerk. Each number described a different gathering.

They settled on the open ground beyond the praetorium gate. Some had come with women or older children. Others had carried only cloaks, bread, and skins. They bore no weapons Claudia could see. That did not prove none were hidden, and Rufus's officers searched the men admitted to speak.

Pontius received Hanan and four elders on the first afternoon. They asked for removal. He answered that imperial images within a Roman fortress violated no Temple boundary. They came out without raising their voices.

Yaakov sat three rows behind the gate in the gray shawl Tamar had described.

"He expected a hearing to change something," Tamar said from the wall. "Now he says they must show that refusal also has a cost."

"How long will he stay?"

"He told his wife five days of bread would be enough. She told him pride eats more than bread. I do not know which of them is right."

That answer was more useful than Tamar's old certainty would have been. Claudia asked whether Yaakov would carry a question to the elders: if Pontius allowed water and bread through the side court, would the crowd accept it without treating the provision as a promise of surrender?

Tamar looked at her sharply. "You want them fed so they can petition your husband longer."

"I want old people kept from falling ill at a gate he controls."

"And you want the gate kept clear."

"Yes."

Tamar agreed to ask her aunt to send word. The answer returned after dark. Some petitioners feared taking Roman provisions would let Pontius call them dependents. Others said refusing water proved nothing. The elders would accept access for private sellers if soldiers did not distribute the food.

Claudia took that narrow agreement to Philotas. The main court remained closed by Pontius's order, but the household's eastern service gate had authority to admit suppliers. She directed Philotas to extend market hours and waive the palace stall fee for sellers carrying bread, water, or lamp oil to the outer ground.

"The guard will say you are evading the prefect's order," he said.

"The order closes the main court and forbids palace provisions. We are admitting sellers through a household gate."

It was the kind of distinction Pontius used. Claudia disliked how readily it came to her.

Philotas demanded written authority. She gave it under her household seal and charged the lost fees to her allowance. By morning, two Roman servants had complained that local petitioners received a privilege denied to veterans' families waiting on ordinary business. Claudia ordered the same fee waiver for anyone buying necessities at the service gate. The expense doubled.

Pontius learned before noon.

"You opened another gate."

"To traders, under guard. No petitioner enters the court. Your officers keep the main gate."

"You have made endurance easier."

"Hunger would not remove the grievance. It would give you sick people outside your wall."

He let the market continue through sunset and ordered it closed at night. Claudia had reduced one danger at financial and household cost. She had not changed his answer.

## Five Days

The first day ended without violence. On the second, officers walked the rows while a proclamation offered safe departure and promised punishment for obstruction. Few left.

On the third, an officer's report claimed the gathering had begun to thin. Marcus placed it beside the service-gate receipts. Bread purchases had risen. Tamar reported fresh arrivals from two inland towns. The three records could all be true if the front ranks changed while the camp grew beyond the officer's assigned ground.

Claudia showed Pontius the discrepancy before his evening council.

"Your officer counted the people within sight of the gate," she said. "The sellers served those camped along the north wall as well. Tamar knows who arrived, though she cannot count them. The protest is spreading around the limit of the report."

He sent Rufus to inspect the full perimeter. Rufus returned after dark and confirmed that departure from the front had concealed arrivals elsewhere.

On the fourth day, Pontius sent soldiers armed beneath their cloaks. They read the proclamation again. The crowd listened, prayed in scattered groups, argued among themselves, and stayed. A few men cursed the soldiers. Others silenced them. Claudia could not tell whether discipline came from shared purpose, fear of giving Rome a pretext, authority among the elders, or all three.

Yaakov was struck at the shoulder when a shield line cleared the service road. Tamar learned of it from a neighbor and asked to leave the household before her duties ended.

Philotas objected. Two kitchen workers were already absent because relatives had joined the petitioners.

"Go," Claudia told Tamar. "Return when you know whether he needs a physician."

She ordered the absence entered as paid household leave. Marcus recorded the reason in a separate line, visible to Philotas and any later auditor. The protection could not be mistaken for an accident.

On the fifth morning, Yaakov remained at the gate with his arm bound. Tamar reported that he could lift the hand and had refused to go home. She did not claim the crowd would never leave.

"He says they have agreed to remain through today," she said. "After that, some expect the prefect to act. They disagree about what he will do."

Marcus brought the military schedule before midday. Rufus had ordered three companies to drill at the stadium before dawn on the sixth day. The public audience list showed a formal hearing there one hour later. The messenger order to the elders had not yet left the records room.

Claudia understood enough to be afraid and too little to accuse Pontius of a massacre he might not intend. She compared the schedule with Rufus's request for medical litters and the armory issue for drawn blades. Then she chose what to take to her husband: the crowd's growth beyond the gate count, Yaakov's refusal after injury, the elders' uncertainty, the litters, and the timing of the soldiers.

She did not take Tamar's name into the room.

Pontius listened without interruption.

"They may believe the stadium is another hearing," Claudia said. "If you mean only to frighten them, know what your own reports now show. Injury has sent some home. It has made others more determined. They are divided about many things. They remain agreed on the standards."

"Rufus told you the plan?"

"The schedules told me soldiers and petitioners are ordered to the same place. The armory told me what the soldiers will carry."

His face hardened at the reach of her comparison. "Household access does not include command."

"I have given no command."

"You opened a gate."

"And you allowed it because sick petitioners would have worsened your choice. I am asking you to enter tomorrow knowing that the crowd has prepared for suffering, though no one I trust can say how far each person will go."

The uncertainty held him longer than a polished warning would have.

He ordered the Syrian dispatch case reopened. Into it he placed Rufus's perimeter correction, the service-gate account, and a brief note stating that the petitioners had endured five days without general disorder. The first memorandum remained on its way. This later account could not erase it.

Pontius kept the stadium order.

Claudia left his study knowing she had changed what he would carry into the sixth day. She had also shown him how far her household channels reached. Before dawn, soldiers and petitioners would enter the stadium under orders only he controlled.
