# Chapter 12: Herodias's Court

## The City on the Graves

Tiberias announced itself before the road did, new stone rising along the western shore of the lake. Tamar had warned that old tombs were said to have been disturbed during construction and that some observant families avoided the city. She did not know how many. The objection itself mattered because Antipas had built a capital whose splendor could be read as impurity.

Pontius had called the visit a courtesy owed between neighboring authorities, prompted by a border dispute over grazing rights that a season of letters had failed to settle and that Antipas himself had finally proposed to close in person, over a shared table, in the manner two men trained to Roman method understood better than any formal arbitration. Claudia had not asked to come. He had asked her to, in a tone that surprised her with how much it wanted an answer rather than assumed one, and she had understood, accepting, that the request was itself a kind of repair attempted after the silence that had followed the riot, a small offered normalcy neither of them was prepared to name as such.

He also gave her work. Antipas's letters blamed Judaean herders for crossing a seasonal line. Petitions reaching Caesarea blamed the tetrarch's estate agents for moving boundary stones toward the west after each rain.

"Learn which households buy the disputed wool," Pontius said. "The men's briefing names pasture. The purchase lists may name who profits."

Claudia brought Marcus's linked copies of three petitions and left the claimants' names sealed. Her objective was to determine whether the dispute belonged to hungry herders, estate policy, or both before Pontius traded enforcement rights for a false peace.

The tetrarch received them in a colonnaded hall modeled on Rome's grand houses. Antipas, compact and watchful in Roman dress, greeted Pontius warmly before servants and secretaries. He praised the aqueduct loudly enough for the room to carry the compliment beyond their private meeting.

The grazing dispute occupied the men for an hour. Pontius argued figures and dated petitions. Antipas conceded small points while refusing any admission about the moved stones. His deference preserved cordiality and left the disputed ground in his agents' hands.

Antipas's steward later supplied Claudia with wool purchases from two royal warehouses. One estate named in the Caesarea petitions had doubled deliveries after the boundary stones moved. The steward called it a good season. A kitchen buyer said the court had ordered cheaper wool from the disputed tract. Neither knew who moved a stone.

The formal evening briefing also mentioned John. Antipas's secretary described confinement at Machaerus as a temporary measure against gatherings near roads and crossings. The paper named no charge concerning Herodias's marriage and no plan for trial or release.

## Wool and Boundary Stones

Claudia asked to see the purchase tablets. Antipas's steward offered summaries. She requested originals and received only the current year.

The warehouse purchases rose after the disputed boundary shifted. Payments went through a land agent named in one Judaean petition, though the estate itself appeared under a royal steward's mark. The chain proved profit and concealed ownership. It did not prove a stone had been moved unlawfully.

Marcus compared witness hands while Claudia questioned the household buyer. The woman knew which wool arrived coarse, which held dye, and which estate demanded payment in advance. She did not know the legal boundary.

"Who told you the western pasture belonged to the estate?" Claudia asked.

"The steward who set the price."

"Did you see markers?"

"I saw shepherds arguing beside one. It was lying flat."

An estate clerk interrupted. The marker had fallen in rain and been restored where surveyors placed it years before. He produced a survey copy without a witness date. Marcus found that its boundary description used a stream name absent from the older petitions.

The buyer said local shepherds used another name for the same stream. The clerk said there were two streams. Neither could show Claudia both on the ground.

She asked for permission to inspect the disputed tract on the return journey. Antipas's steward declined because the rulers were settling the matter that day. Delay, he said, would invite new trespass.

The refusal became part of her report. A settlement reached before inspection favored whoever currently held the pasture.

At midday, a maid serving the women's rooms mentioned that Herodias's household had ordered new winter hangings from the same warehouse. When Claudia asked whether the order predated the boundary dispute, the maid withdrew the claim and said she might have confused the seller.

Claudia gave her no name in Marcus's notes. The maid's position made access credible and correction prudent. It supplied a direction for the tablets, not proof.

The order was there, placed six weeks after the marker was reported moved. Herodias's rooms had benefited from the cheaper wool. Claudia could not tell whether Herodias knew the source. Household use connected her to the profit without establishing that she directed the land agent.

## What the Court Watched

Herodias joined them for the evening banquet with every expression prepared. She took the couch beside Claudia and began asking questions before the first cup was cleared.

"I expected someone homesick for Rome," Herodias said.

"I have been here three years. Whatever homesickness I arrived with has had time to wear itself into something more useful."

"Useful," Herodias repeated. "Then you know what your husband hears before he decides."

It was a question presented as courtesy. "Sometimes."

"The aqueduct must have tested the arrangement."

"I would not call it a lesson anyone learned gladly."

"My husband has confined a man who condemns our marriage," Herodias said. "Your household has heard of him."

Claudia kept her face as still as she had learned, across three years in this province, to keep it. "I have heard something of him. A preacher at the river."

"A preacher at the river."

"A prisoner at Machaerus now. The secretary calls it temporary." Herodias glanced toward Antipas. "Temporary things acquire followers. Your husband learned that delay has a price."

Claudia heard the request beneath the comparison. Herodias wanted Rome's recent violence to make permanent action appear ordinary. "Pontius governs Judaea. Machaerus is your husband's."

"Of course."

Herodias asked next about the grazing petitions. She knew one claimant's village though the formal briefing had omitted it. Claudia gave no names. The conversation had moved from John to land without changing its method: Herodias tested what Claudia carried back to Pontius and offered pressure she could later deny.

## Salome

Herodias's daughter found Claudia later that evening, in the colonnade beyond the hall where the lake's night air offered some relief from the banquet's heat and noise, a young woman perhaps sixteen or seventeen with her mother's composure not yet fully learned, still showing the seams where the performance had to be consciously assembled rather than worn like skin.

"My mother told you about the prisoner," Salome said.

"Does your stepfather want to do something about it?"

"He does not speak of it to me. My mother does."

"What does she ask of you?"

"To be present when guests come. To hear who calls the prisoner prophet and who calls him disturber."

That made Salome part of the household's listening without making her privy to Antipas's decision.

"You speak of him carefully," Claudia said. "More carefully than your mother does."

"I have never seen him," Salome said. "A guard said he does not beg. Another said he shouts through the door. They served different watches."

"Does Antipas visit him?"

"I saw my stepfather leave for Machaerus twice. My mother says he went to question the prisoner. The captain says he inspected the fortress."

"That does not sound like a man your mother should fear losing an argument to."

"I do not know what he wants," Salome said. "I know my mother wants him silent."

The formal briefing called confinement temporary. Herodias treated delay as danger. Salome knew household uses and guard stories. None revealed what Antipas would decide.

## What the Banquet Concealed

During the final course, Antipas praised Pontius for continuing the aqueduct after unrest. Herodias asked whether Roman patience had limits. Pontius answered that lawful work continued while disturbance ended.

"Then certainty is sometimes merciful," Herodias said.

Antipas changed the subject to racing teams.

The movement was small. Claudia could not know whether he protected John, avoided marital dispute before guests, or rejected a lesson drawn from deaths in another province. She recorded only that Herodias offered the aqueduct as precedent and Antipas refused the comparison in public.

Later, the captain of the household guard delivered the next day's escort schedule. Claudia asked whether guards rotated between Tiberias and Machaerus.

"Some."

"Do they report on the prisoner?"

"They report on the fortress."

"Does Antipas question him?"

"The tetrarch inspects his holdings."

The captain's answer matched the official record and contradicted none of Salome's observations. It removed John from every sentence without denying the visits.

Marcus learned from a Greek secretary that three Machaerus reports had arrived that month. One concerned provisions, one a change of watch, and one a group turned away from the fortress road. Claudia was not permitted to read them.

She asked whether the group carried weapons.

"The report would have gone to the men if they did."

"How many?"

"Enough to be turned away."

It was the same answer Roman officers used when a number had not been preserved or was inconvenient to share.

Before retiring, Claudia wrote separate lines for what each source could know:

- The secretary could prove the language Antipas's court would defend.
- The steward and buyer could prove household purchases and the estate agent's profit.
- The maid could point to a purchase and had reason to retreat from attention.
- Herodias could state her pressure and shape what Claudia carried to Pontius.
- Salome could report household tasks, departures, and guard talk.
- The captain could confirm fortress movement while protecting its purpose.

The list contained no mind, plot, or future act. It showed a court preparing to survive several possible decisions.

The estate clerk discovered that Claudia had asked about the winter hangings. He demanded the maid's name, saying a false statement about royal purchases could cost a household supplier his contract.

Claudia refused. She produced the purchase tablet instead. Its date and seller made the maid unnecessary to the question of profit.

"Someone directed you to it."

"Your summaries omitted it. The original did not."

The clerk threatened to report obstruction to Herodias. Claudia invited him to do so in her presence. He chose silence, which protected his own incomplete summary.

Claudia arranged for the maid's next three days of service to remain in the women's rooms rather than the record court. She had no authority to command Antipas's staff, but Salome could alter her own attendants. Salome agreed without asking why.

"My mother will notice if you make a secret of it," she warned.

"Then call it hospitality to a Roman guest."

The protection might draw the attention it meant to prevent. It also kept the clerk from questioning the maid alone before Claudia left.

Herodias noticed before the evening lamps were lit.

"You have borrowed one of my servants," she said when Claudia entered the women's dining room.

"Your daughter assigned an attendant to me."

"After you questioned my buyer."

Claudia could deny neither action without leaving the maid exposed as the only remaining explanation. "I asked about a purchase made from land under dispute."

"My hangings determine the border now?"

"They determine who gained while the border was uncertain."

Herodias dismissed the other attendants except Salome. The maid remained beyond the door with Claudia's cloak. Her safety lasted only while Claudia's rank made a private punishment inconvenient.

"My steward says you concealed a false accusation," Herodias said.

"I concealed a servant's name. The tablet confirms the purchase."

"A Roman governor's wife protects servants in another ruler's house and calls it inquiry."

"I asked for the tablets because Pontius asked me to learn who bought the wool."

That answer spent part of Pontius's courtesy. Herodias could now tell Antipas that the household visit had carried an undisclosed investigation. Claudia had protected the maid by making herself, and through herself Pontius, responsible for the question.

Salome spoke before her mother answered. "The attendant stays with our guest until departure. I gave the order."

Herodias looked at her daughter. "Then you will answer for any work left undone."

"Yes."

The cost moved downward despite Claudia's effort. She could protect the maid for a night; she could not remove Salome from her mother's authority or erase the resentment from the household.

Herodias restored her smile when the attendants returned. "I hope Judaea rewards such care."

Claudia understood the warning as broadly as it had been offered. Herodias had learned that household records and lesser servants entered Claudia's judgments. Future access at Tiberias would be narrower.

Before departure Claudia gave the maid enough coin to cover three days' wages if Herodias's steward dismissed her. The woman refused the first offer.

"Coin will look like payment for a tale," she said.

Claudia placed it with Salome instead, designated for wages only if service was lost. Salome accepted the burden with visible reluctance. The arrangement preserved a remedy without pretending Claudia could promise safety.

## The Ride Back

On the road home Claudia gave Pontius the wool purchases first. The royal warehouse had profited after the stones moved, though no witness proved who moved them.

Pontius reopened the settlement he had initialed. He sent Antipas a condition: both administrations would replace the disputed markers under paired witnesses before either punished a herder for crossing them. Antipas accepted because refusal would expose the warehouse gain. Claudia's household inquiry changed the agreement without deciding the boundary itself.

Pontius did not accept her inference at once. He asked whether the higher deliveries could reflect better pasture, whether the fallen marker might explain the shift, and whether a cheaper warehouse had simply won Herodias's order.

"All are possible," Claudia said. "That is why the stones must be witnessed before fines begin."

He compared the undated survey copy with the old petitions. Marcus's note about the stream names prevented him from treating the map as a settled line. Pontius dictated the condition at the next road station and sent a mounted courier back to Tiberias before Antipas's version reached the local officers.

The answer caught them two days later. Antipas agreed to paired witnesses and proposed one surveyor from each court. He denied that warehouse profit had influenced the earlier settlement.

Pontius preserved the denial beside the purchase totals. "He accepted the remedy while disputing the reason."

"A durable settlement?"

"A usable one."

The exchange restored the markers to the realm of observed ground. It did not resolve the older claim to pasture, which remained for the surveyors.

Claudia then told him what the inquiry had cost. Herodias knew the visit had included more than courtesy. Salome had assumed responsibility for the maid's reassignment, and the household might close its records on their next visit.

"You used my request when she challenged you," Pontius said.

"It was your request."

"It was meant to inform our settlement. You made it a quarrel in her rooms."

"The clerk was searching for the servant who led us to a purchase his summary omitted."

Pontius considered that. He would reimburse no secret wage payment from provincial funds. Claudia charged the possible expense to her household allowance and asked Marcus to keep the woman's name outside the grazing file.

"Then the record cannot prove how you found the tablet," Pontius said.

"It can prove the tablet. It can also prove the summary omitted it."

He accepted the separation. The remedy protected a source and weakened the account of Claudia's method. That was the price of leaving the maid more than a useful name in a Roman file.

Then she gave Pontius four versions of John: temporary confinement in the formal paper, urgent danger in Herodias's conversation, contradictory guard stories through Salome, and marketplace claims already linking the preacher to the marriage.

"It is Antipas's jurisdiction," Pontius said.

"Yes. I am asking you to remember what fear is being named before the reason changes."

"You think he will kill the man."

"I think his household is preparing several explanations for whatever he does."

Pontius asked which account she trusted.

"The formal paper proves what the secretary will defend. Herodias proves what she wants me to carry. Salome proves the household listens. None proves Antipas's purpose."

He ordered Marcus's Chapter 8 comparison linked to Claudia's Tiberias notes under Machaerus and Antipas. The record would remain outside a Roman security file unless a border consequence arose.

Claudia had left with an answer about wool and no answer about the prisoner. The uncertainty itself had sharpened. In Antipas's court, fear traveled as hospitality, briefing, servant talk, and family pressure, each version ready for a different listener.
