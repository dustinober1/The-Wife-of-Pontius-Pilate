# Chapter 9: Corban

*POV: Pontius*

## The Problem of Water

Jerusalem's problem was one Pontius understood better than anything the priesthood had yet brought him. The city held too many people for the water that reached it. He had the figures from his own engineers before the pilgrims' complaints reached his desk, laid out in the flat language he trusted above every other kind. At Passover the population within the walls swelled to three or four times its ordinary count. In a bad year the existing channel from the pools at Solomon ran dry by the festival's second week, cisterns were rationed by household, and the Temple's own basins were refilled from hand-carried jars drawn at wells the priesthood guarded against the crowds.

He had walked the old channel himself the previous summer in the plain traveling cloak he preferred for inspections that did not require the weight of office. He came away satisfied. Here at last was a problem shaped like the ones he knew how to solve. Judaea's people could not be measured, weighed, or made to yield to a ledger. Water could. A channel run from the springs near Bethlehem, cut through twenty miles of hill country by the same engineering that had watered every city Rome bothered to keep, would settle in a single season what eleven years of Gratus's caution had left to fester.

The only question was where the money came from. Pontius turned it over for most of a month and arrived at an answer so obvious he wondered why no prefect before him had reached for it. The Temple treasury held vast sums, offerings gathered from Jews across the empire and left largely idle between festivals, guarded with a zeal that struck him, examining the account, as hoarding dressed up in piety. A portion of it, spent on water that would serve the Temple's own basins along with every household in the city, hardly counted as confiscation. It was the treasury doing something useful at last with money otherwise locked against a use no one could name.

"It is their money spent on their water," he said to Rufus, who had come on the inspection and understood engineering well enough to follow the reasoning without sharing his confidence in how it would be received. "I fail to see the objection they will find in it."

"They will find one, Prefect. They generally do."

"Then they will find one to an aqueduct that fills their own basins, which will at least have the virtue of being a foolish objection rather than a reasonable one." Pontius said it lightly. The figures stayed with him on the ride back to Caesarea, arranging themselves into a proposal. By the time he reached his study he knew he would put it to Caiaphas within the week.

He did not intend to repeat the standards. There would be no order completed at night and defended after discovery, no five days in which opposition could settle into ranks. The legal use of the money would be established before work began. The first public notice would travel with the first surveyors. If objection came, it would meet a project already authorized by both institutions and divided into contracts too numerous to reverse through one demand.

Claudia read the proposed schedule at supper.

"You have allowed one day between the notice and the first excavation."

"Enough time to receive the notice."

"Not enough to answer it."

"The answer belongs in the agreement. I will not hold a public hearing on whether Jerusalem should have water."

"Who signs for the treasury?"

"Caiaphas."

"And if he gives you access without putting his name on your wording?"

Pontius had assumed the high priest's interest in the water would overcome reluctance to be seen authorizing sacred expense. Claudia had identified the place where shared advantage might become one-sided evidence.

He ordered the prefectural secretary to prepare three instruments. The first stated the engineering purpose and maximum sum. The second required Temple and Roman witnesses for each release. The third named responsibility for public notice. Caiaphas could dispute language. He could not be permitted to vanish from the paper entirely.

## The High Priest's Account

Caiaphas received him in a chamber off the Temple's outer court. Neither man named the compromise aloud. A prefect summoned to the inner precincts would have read as submission, and a high priest summoned to the Antonia as the same thing reversed. Pontius had met him twice before, at the handover of the sacred vestments each festival required, and had come away with the impression of a man who survived by calculation. Whatever his reservations about the office, he found that almost restful. A man who calculated could be reasoned with in the language of calculation. A zealot could not.

"The Prefect brings an engineering matter to a Temple audience," Caiaphas said, after the formalities. "I confess I had expected a different kind of visit."

"I bring you a solution to a problem your own priesthood has complained of at every festival I've presided over. Insufficient water for the crowds, insufficient water for the basins, cisterns rationed by households who came a hundred miles to make an offering and found the city too dry to receive them properly." Pontius laid the survey before him, the figures rendered as plainly as he could make them without insulting the man's intelligence. "A channel from the springs near Bethlehem, engineered to Roman standard, would end the shortage within two years of construction. I ask the treasury to fund the portion my own provincial revenues cannot cover, since the water will serve the Temple's own courts as fully as it serves any Roman garrison."

Caiaphas studied the figures with the unhurried attention of a man who had learned long ago never to answer a Roman request before he had located every angle from which it might later be turned against him. "The korbanas is not an ordinary treasury, Prefect. It is offerings dedicated to God. Corban, we call the gift once it is made: a thing set apart, no longer available to the ordinary uses of men, however worthy those uses might otherwise seem."

"I am not asking you to spend it on a fortress or a garrison road. I am asking you to spend it on water that will fill your own basins at every festival from now until the channel silts up centuries from now. If the offering is dedicated to God's house, I fail to see how water for God's house lies outside its purpose."

"You fail to see it because you are not the one who will answer for the spending, in this life or whatever the priesthood believes comes after it." Caiaphas said it without heat, in the same measured voice he had used throughout. Pontius understood that he was watching a man weigh one political cost against another, whatever vocabulary he chose for the weighing. "There will be those among the people who call it theft dressed as engineering, however sound your figures. There will be those among the priesthood who call it worse. I must decide whether the water is worth the accusation that will follow the spending of it."

"And what do you decide."

Caiaphas was silent long enough that Pontius began composing the alternate proposal he would need if this one failed: provincial revenues alone, a longer channel, a slower schedule, a solution half as good at twice the cost. Then the high priest set the survey down and folded his hands over it. Pontius read the gesture as assent already reached and held back for the look of deliberation.

"I decide that the work may proceed if the officers responsible for the dedicated gifts judge the use lawful," Caiaphas said. "My overseers will examine each release beside yours. They will attest the sum and its destination."

"That is supervision. I asked for authority."

"You ask me to make one sentence answer every teacher who may dispute a use of offerings given for many purposes. I cannot."

"Yet your officers can open the treasury."

"If they accept the purpose and the custodians release the sum."

The difference had been chosen carefully. Caiaphas would permit, oversee, and benefit. He would not state that his office had converted sacred gifts into an ordinary civic fund.

Pontius put forward the first instrument. Caiaphas struck the phrase *authorized by the high priest* and substituted *presented to the custodians with the high priest's knowledge*. On the second, he accepted paired witnesses. On the third, he refused a joint proclamation.

"The Temple may announce that work has begun to improve the festival supply," Caiaphas said. "Rome may announce its engineering. If we write one notice, every word becomes a quarrel before stone is cut."

"If we write two, you may praise the water and leave me the treasury."

"And you may praise lawful authority and leave me the anger."

Neither man denied the possibility.

Pontius required the liaison created after Hanan's delegation to carry copies between the Temple household and the prefectural office. Each release would show the Temple custodian's mark, the Roman paymaster's mark, amount, destination, and witnesses. Caiaphas accepted after adding that a mark attested delivery, not agreement with every legal claim made for the project.

"Then we are settled on the money," Pontius said.

"We are settled on a procedure by which money may be released," Caiaphas answered. "We will learn whether it survives its first public reading."

Pontius left with access to the treasury and without the clean authorization he had meant to obtain.

## The First Release

The first request covered survey wages, stone, tools, and guards for the springs. The Temple custodian approved stone and labor, rejected guards, and returned the tablet through the liaison with a note that sacred gifts should not maintain Roman force.

Pontius moved the guard expense to provincial revenue. The division improved the document. No opponent could claim the whole work served soldiers, and no Roman auditor could say he had charged imperial security to the Temple.

The second request stalled because a quarry named by the engineer belonged to a priestly household. Caiaphas's overseer proposed another source at higher cost. Pontius suspected patronage. The overseer claimed impurity concerns near the first road. Neither could prove the other's motive.

For three days the survey crews waited. Pontius saw the standards lesson returning in another form: delay gave every clerk and custodian power to enlarge a decision already made.

He accepted the higher price for the first month and required both quarry rates preserved in the account. The work began. The compromise cost money and left a record that could support either claim, priestly caution or household profit.

Claudia asked Marcus to link the first releases to petitions concerning wells, land crossed by the channel, and unpaid carts. Pontius allowed her to see the household copies of public claims, not the treasury originals. Her method might warn him where resistance would form. He kept the authority to answer it.

One petition came from farmers near the springs who feared the survey marked land seizure. Another came from Jerusalem potters who wanted contracts. A third bore no names and called the treasury untouchable. The liaison reported that some priests praised the water while denying that offerings could bear the cost.

Pontius accelerated the first visible work. Survey stakes went in before the next council meeting. Contracts were divided among crews so no single stoppage could halt the line. The public notice named the aqueduct's benefit, the paired witnesses, and the custodians' release. It did not say Caiaphas authorized the use. Caiaphas's notice named improved supply and omitted Rome's control of construction.

Both were accurate. Together they revealed the bargain each side might later deny.

## A Contingency for Petition

Rufus asked what force should guard the public notice in Jerusalem.

"Visible men at the works," Pontius said. "Ordinary protection for tools and surveyors."

"And if the protest gathers at your tribunal?"

Pontius had prepared for the question. The standards crowd had been strengthened by five days of watching soldiers arrive, proclaim, threaten, and fail. He would not build another ring of drawn swords around people who could make refusal into spectacle.

"Select men who can pass as laborers and pilgrims. No armor. Clubs beneath their cloaks. Place them along the approaches before the hearing."

Rufus's expression tightened. "Soldiers without uniform lose the protection of being recognized as soldiers."

"They gain the chance to reach the men pressing forward before a line forms."

"A crowd may strike them as private enemies."

"They do not act until the signal. Their purpose is dispersal. Clubs, no blades."

"A club can kill."

"So can a shield line in a narrow street. This keeps force inside the crowd and away from the Temple entrances."

Rufus looked at the construction petitions on the table. "How many men?"

Pontius refused to set a number before knowing the ground. He ordered Rufus to survey exits, approaches, and places where a crowd might compress. The plan would specify who gave the signal, which routes remained open, and where the soldiers re-formed after acting. No officer would improvise a pursuit.

"Put the purpose in the sealed order," Pontius said. "Disperse those pressing the tribunal. Do not punish those leaving. Do not enter the Temple courts."

"And the public notice?"

"It will say the prefect hears petitions under ordinary guard."

Rufus met his eyes. "The guard will not be ordinary."

"The hearing will appear ordinary until someone chooses to break it."

Pontius heard the weakness in the sentence and kept it. Recognition had made the standards threat useless. Concealment would preserve the first advantage and prevent a prolonged confrontation. Technical legality would remain visible. Force would appear only when the crowd created the condition named in the order.

He required the sealed instructions stored with Rufus until the Jerusalem household moved for the next festival. The prefectural secretary would prepare the public hearing notice and a blank dispatch form for any disturbance. The first account could leave before rumor supplied its own cause.

This was what he had learned: time favored the gathering, visible preparation invited a test, and an order should contain its conclusion before opposition discovered where to press.

## What the Crowd Would Call It

Pontius rode back to Caesarea turning the agreement over as he did every successful negotiation. The habit had kept him alive through worse bargains. He found ordinary friction, divided signatures, and a useful channel. He could not make himself believe that clean water running into a city's basins would remain forever defined by the treasury from which the first coin came.

He knew someone would read it otherwise. Someone always did. There would be men in the markets and the colonnades who called it theft the moment the first shovel broke ground, whatever hand had authorized the spending and whosever basins the water filled. A crowd rarely troubled itself with the difference between an act and the story told about it afterward, and a Roman prefect spending sacred money made a better story than a high priest agreeing to the same expense out of plain civic sense. He had learned that much on the stadium benches. A grievance did not have to be accurate to gather men around it. It had to be vivid. A treasury emptied by foreign hands for a foreign engineer's channel would outlast any correction he could issue.

He decided, riding the last mile into Caesarea with the sea below the road, that opposition must meet completed segments and separated accounts rather than a single public choice. The channel itself would become the argument. If the water reached the city before complaint hardened, reversal would cost more than continuation.

At the palace, the liaison's next packet waited. The Temple overseer had marked the first payment complete. Beneath the mark he had written that witness did not imply approval of the prefect's interpretation of dedicated funds.

Pontius ordered the qualification preserved. It proved the overseer had seen the payment. It also preserved the sentence by which the Temple might later claim it had watched Rome take what it never lawfully gave.

The figures were sound. Water would reach the city. The bargain had opened the treasury and left responsibility divided precisely where either side could use it against the other.
