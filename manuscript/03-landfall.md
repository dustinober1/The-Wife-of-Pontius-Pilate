# Chapter 3: Landfall

## The Shore of Judaea

Land announced itself first by smell, dry resin carried across the last miles of water.

Claudia stood at the rail as Caesarea emerged from the morning glare. White stone resolved into towers, then the curved harbor Herod had forced into a coast without natural shelter.

"Volcanic ash beneath the foundations," Pontius said. He had shaved for the arrival, a small act of control after three weeks at sea. "The engineers said it could not be done."

"And it held."

"It held."

He respected structures that resisted failure. Claudia watched the breakwater and wondered which reports had recorded the men lost building it.

Marcus approached with a wax tablet. "The harbor master's boat is coming out. Philotas has sent a reception list, but two names on it do not match the household manifest."

"Which names?"

"A Roman steward called Lucanus and a local kitchen supervisor called Mattan. The shipboard copy lists neither."

"Keep both versions," Claudia said. "Ask who added them and when."

Pontius glanced over. "Already comparing records?"

"The province has begun comparing us."

The city rose behind the harbor: theater, colonnades, temples, warehouses, and straight Roman streets. Beyond them the hills folded inland, crossed by footpaths no surveyor had made.

Caesarea faced the sea and Rome. The country it governed began behind its back.

## The Palace by the Sea

Herod's palace stood on the promontory, part residence and part prefectural headquarters. Salt moved through its corridors despite the incense burned for their arrival.

Philotas received them in the audience hall. Thin, anxious, and careful, he had served three administrations and appeared determined to survive another.

"The records are in the western rooms," he told Pontius. "The household accounts in the lower office. I have ordered the local staff to present themselves before sunset."

"Who added Lucanus and Mattan to the reception list?" Claudia asked.

Philotas blinked. "Lucanus served Gratus's chief secretary. Mattan oversees purchases from the Jewish market. Their names may have been omitted from the ship copy."

"By whom?"

"The copy was prepared before Gratus's departure."

Marcus produced both tablets. "The later list uses a different hand for those two entries."

Philotas looked at Marcus, then at Claudia. "I added them this morning. Lucanus asked that he remain through the transition. Mattan is necessary if the kitchens are to buy meat acceptable to part of the household."

Pontius had already turned toward the records room. "Keep whoever is useful. Dismiss whoever is not."

The instruction seemed simple. Claudia watched Philotas relax and Marcus mark the names. The decision had been delegated without a standard for usefulness, which meant the old hierarchy would decide whom the new household needed.

Claudia spent the afternoon walking the service rooms with Philotas. Roman servants occupied the positions closest to the prefect's person. Greek freedmen controlled records and purchasing. Local hired workers carried water, cleaned courtyards, prepared foods the Roman cooks did not understand, and absorbed blame when schedules failed.

She asked for wage rolls and duty lists. Philotas produced one but not the other. The wage roll named free workers. The duty list mixed them with enslaved staff and marked both with the same symbol.

"Separate them," Claudia said.

"The work is the same."

"The consequences are not. A free worker can leave. An enslaved servant can be punished or sold. I need to know which threat a steward is using when he calls someone disobedient."

Philotas's expression suggested the distinction would create inconvenience. That was precisely why it had been erased.

By sunset, that ambiguity produced its first victim.

## The Kitchen Account

Claudia found shouting in the service court.

A Roman steward stood over a young local woman beside two baskets of fish. One basket had been overturned. Silver coins lay in the wet dust.

"She refused the purchase order," the steward said when he saw Claudia. "Then accused the supplier of cheating the prefect's house. I have dismissed her."

The woman held herself still. "I said the fish were counted twice."

"Your name?" Claudia asked.

"Tamar. I keep the eastern courtyard and assist with market accounts when Mattan is absent."

The steward thrust a tablet forward. "The order bears Mattan's mark. She has no standing to dispute it."

Claudia read the entry. Forty fish from the harbor market, paid at the morning rate. The baskets held perhaps twenty-four.

"Where is Mattan?"

"At his brother's burial," Tamar said. "He will not return until after sunset."

The steward frowned at her speaking without permission. "Which is why Lucanus signed in his place."

Claudia examined the wax. Mattan's usual mark, copied from the household manifest Marcus had shown her, curved inward at the base. This one curved out.

"Marcus."

He came from the passage with his tablet.

"Compare the market order with Mattan's mark on the transition list."

Marcus did so. "They differ, Domina."

Lucanus stepped forward. "A clerk's variation."

"Who carried the coins?" Claudia asked.

A kitchen boy admitted Lucanus had counted them. The fish seller had left before the baskets were opened.

Claudia could prove a false mark and missing goods. She could not prove who had profited. Dismissing Lucanus immediately might disrupt the records transition and provoke every Roman servant who expected local staff to bear blame first.

"Lucanus remains confined to the lower office until Pontius decides the question of the false mark," she said. "The purchase is entered as disputed. Tamar is retained."

The steward stared at her. "On whose authority?"

"Mine over the household accounts. Marcus, record that I suspended the dismissal and the reason. Send a copy to Philotas and one to the prefect's secretary."

That made the choice costly. It placed Claudia's name in the record before she knew whether Pontius would support her.

Lucanus was escorted away. The Roman kitchen servants watched in silence. Tamar knelt to gather the coins.

"Leave them," Claudia said. "Marcus will count them with you."

Tamar's eyes narrowed. "With me?"

"You challenged an order and were correct about the count. That does not make you correct about everything. It makes you the person who saw this."

The kitchen staff objected to Marcus entering the market account because he belonged to Claudia's Roman household and had no knowledge of local prices. Tamar objected to being treated as the sole witness because Lucanus could later claim she had changed the numbers.

Claudia solved the dispute by dividing the authority. Tamar would name the rate, seller, and expected quantity. Marcus would record the testimony, count the coins, and preserve both marks. Neither could establish the whole account alone.

Together Tamar and Marcus counted the silver. Marcus recorded amount, source, and witness. Tamar identified the market rate and named two sellers who could confirm it. Their functions did not overlap. Marcus preserved the administrative trail. Tamar supplied local knowledge of the transaction.

The missing money equaled twelve fish at the listed rate.

Pontius summoned Claudia before the evening meal. Lucanus had served the previous administration's secretary and knew where several unindexed files had been stored. Removing him immediately would slow the prefect's review.

"You have made a household theft into an official question," Pontius said.

"He used a false mark on the prefect's account."

"You could have replaced the fish and dismissed the woman quietly."

"Then every Roman steward in the house would know a local worker may be punished for finding a false account."

Pontius tapped the disputed tablet. "And now they know my wife may suspend them."

"Only when the goods, mark, and money fail to agree."

He disliked the precedent. He liked theft less. He authorized Philotas to search Lucanus's desk and warned Claudia that her household authority did not extend to prefectural records.

"Then keep the two systems separate," she said. "Do not let a man use one to steal from the other."

The search found the missing silver wrapped inside an old shipping order. Pontius upheld the suspension.

## The First Rota

The next practical dispute arrived before Lucanus's inquiry was complete. Philotas had copied Gratus's meal schedule without noting the Sabbath. The list placed Tamar, Mattan, and six local workers on kitchen and water duty from sunset to sunset. Two Roman servants had already warned them that refusal would be entered as absence.

Claudia asked Tamar what work could continue and what could be prepared beforehand. Tamar answered for herself and named the limits of her knowledge. Some workers observed more strictly than others. One would carry a sick child but not a wine jar. Another would not light a fire but would serve food already prepared. Mattan followed rules Tamar's family did not.

"You cannot give me one rule," Claudia said.

"There is one law. People still live it differently."

Claudia called the affected workers into the service court in pairs, with Tamar present only when they requested her. Marcus recorded each duty they could perform, each they would refuse, and whether preparation before sunset solved the problem. Philotas objected that individual arrangements would destroy the rota.

"Then build the rota from duties instead of assumptions," Claudia said.

The solution required Roman staff to cover two late watches and required the kitchens to bake bread earlier. Sulpicia complained that the Roman women were being made to carry provincial customs on their backs. Claudia answered by taking responsibility for the schedule and adding a small wage supplement from her household allowance for the two additional watches.

That choice kept the kitchens operating and prevented six workers from being marked absent. It also taught Philotas that Claudia would spend money to preserve a local practice, a fact he could use later when arguing against her decisions.

## A Woman Named Tamar

Later, Claudia found Tamar in the eastern courtyard beside a basin of green water.

"It draws mosquitoes," Tamar said. "The last household called it restful."

"Drain it."

Tamar looked surprised by the speed of the answer.

"I am free to leave this house," she said after a moment. "Lucanus spoke as if dismissal meant sale. It does not. But work under the prefect protects my aunt's household from hunger. A Roman accusation follows a person farther than a Roman apology."

The correction landed cleanly. Claudia had understood the risk, though incompletely. Tamar possessed freedom Marcus did not. She also lived under an occupying household's power to damage reputation, wages, and family security.

"Then the record will state that the dismissal was withdrawn because the count supported your objection," Claudia said. "It will not call the matter a misunderstanding."

"Why?"

"Because Lucanus used a false mark. Because the next steward should know I compare the account with the goods."

Tamar opened the basin channel. Water moved toward the sea.

"There will be other disputes," she said. "Sabbath work. Meat bought from sellers some of us cannot use. Vessels mixed in the kitchen. Romans will call them refusals because refusal is the part they can see."

"Tell me the practical consequence before the order is given."

"I may not know until I hear the order."

"Then tell me after. Plainly."

Tamar studied her. "And you will believe me?"

"I will compare what you say with what happened. Belief without comparison would make you responsible for more than you can know."

A faint smile appeared. "That is a Roman answer."

"Probably."

"It may still be useful."

The following morning, Mattan returned from burial and reviewed the revised rota. He found two errors. Claudia had scheduled bread preparation during a period his household treated differently from Tamar's. She corrected the tablet in front of him and ordered Marcus to preserve the first version beneath the second rather than smoothing away the mistake.

"Why keep the error?" Philotas asked.

"Because we may repeat it if we pretend it was never made."

Tamar heard the answer from the doorway. She said nothing, but later gave Marcus the name of a potter whose vessels could prevent another kitchen dispute. The information came as a practical exchange, not allegiance.

The outcome was small. Tamar kept her place. Twelve fish and a handful of coins returned to the account. Yet Claudia's intervention angered part of the Roman staff and tied her name to the protection of a local worker on their first day.

Before supper, Tamar returned with a correction. The twelve missing fish had not all been intended for the palace. Four were customarily delivered to a veterans' dining room under an arrangement left by Gratus. The practice had never appeared in the household account because Lucanus collected payment from both kitchens.

Claudia could have concealed the complication and preserved a cleaner victory. Instead she sent Marcus to amend the report. Lucanus had stolen, but Tamar's first calculation had been incomplete.

"You changed the record against your own accusation," Tamar said.

"Against part of it. A true charge does not become stronger by keeping a false detail."

That answer earned no friendship. It earned a longer look and, later, the first warning Tamar offered without being asked: several local workers planned to leave before the coming Sabbath rather than risk punishment for refusing duties. Claudia revised the rota before the resignations occurred. The household remained staffed, a limited outcome improved by information that would otherwise have arrived as disruption.

At supper, Philotas warned her quietly that Roman servants would now bring every quarrel to her.

"Then they will learn to bring evidence," Claudia said.

Across the room Tamar served wine she did not drink. Her reserve remained. Trust had not appeared because Claudia wished for it.

Still, when a kitchen order touched local custom, Tamar now had a reason to speak before harm became routine. Marcus had a defined method for preserving what she reported. Claudia had gained conditional access to two different kinds of information.

The next day a Roman supplier refused to deal with Mattan and demanded a Roman intermediary. Claudia declined to restore Lucanus and assigned Philotas to witness the purchase instead. The supplier raised his price. Tamar knew another seller inland, but she warned that his fish would arrive later and less fresh. Claudia chose delay over rewarding the refusal. Supper changed, Pontius complained, and the household learned that her protections carried visible costs.

Tamar did not thank her. "You can afford to make a point with one meal," she said. "The seller can afford to wait for another Roman house. Mattan cannot afford many days when no one buys through him."

Claudia had treated the dispute as precedent. Tamar forced her to see the worker inside it. Claudia authorized Mattan to buy directly from three approved sellers and set a seven-day review of prices. Marcus would compare the purchases. Tamar would report whether the approved list excluded sellers for reasons the Roman records missed.

The arrangement might fail. It depended on honest counts, changing market prices, and people who had little reason to trust one another. Still, it gave Mattan work and reduced one steward's ability to control access. Claudia had changed another small outcome, this time after correcting her own first response.

She had also shown the household exactly where pressure might be applied to her.
