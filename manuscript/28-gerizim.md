# Chapter 28: Gerizim

*POV: Pontius*

Pontius intended to stop the gathering at the road.

The order was limited in purpose and plain in language. Cavalry would reach the ascent before the main body. Infantry would hold the approaches. The commander would demand dispersal, seize the man promising sacred vessels, and detain anyone displaying arms after the warning. Families and ordinary travelers could withdraw.

This was how a prefect prevented blood: arrive before a crowd discovered its strength.

The first field message reached Caesarea near midday. The cavalry had found people already collecting around the village below the mountain. The commander's clerk used the word *multitude* and supplied no count. Men with swords had been reported near the prophet. Others carried staffs, agricultural knives, or nothing visible.

The report did not say whether terms had been delivered.

Pontius sent the clerk back to ask.

"The commander may be engaged before your question reaches him," the legal scribe said.

"Then his second report will answer."

He ordered the council room opened and had the Samaritan notices arranged by arrival. The first named a deceiver. The second described sacred vessels said to have been hidden by Moses. The third warned of arms.

Claudia's request lay beside them: distinguish ascent from seizure; separate households from men prepared to resist; wait for the second station report.

He moved it to the private side of the table.

The later station tablet had arrived before the troops encountered the gathering, though after his order left. It confirmed weapons among the travelers and refused to estimate how many. That was enough to justify readiness. It did not settle the character of the whole body.

No commander ever received certainty before movement. Waiting did not preserve a pure choice. It surrendered the road, the height, and the first account of the event to whoever arrived fastest.

At the standards protest Pontius had allowed endurance to become authority. Five days gave petitioners time to act as one body and Rome time to appear unable to move them. At the aqueduct riot, force hidden among the crowd had broken resistance before a formation could harden. During Passover, he had let procedures multiply until every exit looked like surrender to someone.

Gerizim required an earlier boundary.

He summoned the officer responsible for Samaritan affairs, a decurion too old for field command and too experienced to flatter cleanly.

"Would pilgrims carry swords to the mountain?" Pontius asked.

"Some travelers carry them on the roads."

"Would worshipers answer a promise of buried vessels?"

"Some would. Some would come to watch those who did."

"Would rebels use the same promise?"

"They might."

"Then what distinguishes them?"

"What they do when challenged."

Pontius looked at the empty space in the first field report. "The commander was ordered to challenge them."

"Yes, Prefect."

The old officer did not add that Pontius still lacked the answer.

## The First Account

The second field messenger arrived with blood on his sandal that was not his own.

The cavalry had blocked the forward road. People behind continued pressing into the confined ground because they could not see the halt. An officer called for dispersal in Greek and through a local interpreter. Those nearest heard. Whether the warning passed through the rear could not be established.

A group near the front refused to surrender its weapons. One man threw a stone or sling missile. The messenger had seen something strike a horse and had not seen the throw.

The horse reared. Cavalry moved.

"Moved where?" Pontius asked.

"Into the armed group."

"At the commander's order?"

"The trumpet sounded."

"Which signal?"

"Advance, Prefect."

"Advance or charge?"

The messenger swallowed. "The calls are close when men are shouting."

Pontius ordered water brought and made him begin again.

The cavalry drove the front rank from the road. People trying to retreat collided with those still approaching. Infantry closed from the side to prevent movement toward the ascent. Some in the crowd fought. Some ran. Some fell beneath horses or were struck while trapped between bodies.

The prophet's location was uncertain. One prisoner said he had remained behind the armed men. Another said he had already fled. The commander believed leaders were attempting to reach the mountain by another path.

"Deaths?"

"Some."

"Soldiers?"

"Two cavalry wounded. One infantryman struck in the mouth."

"Among the crowd?"

"The commander will send a count."

The messenger's avoidance gave Pontius an estimate larger than *some*.

He asked whether women and children had been present.

"Families were on the road."

"At the point of resistance?"

"Behind it. Also among people to the side."

"Were they given a withdrawal route?"

"The cavalry opened the southern edge after the first movement."

After.

Pontius dictated an immediate order: cease pursuit beyond the ascent approaches; open two routes for unarmed departure; treat the injured without inquiry until stable; separate prisoners taken with weapons from those seized in the press; send the prophet alive if captured.

The instructions were sound. They also proved the field had passed beyond the action he first authorized.

The legal scribe began a preliminary summary:

*Roman forces blocking an unlawful armed ascent encountered resistance after a warning to disperse.*

"Strike unlawful," Pontius said.

The scribe looked up.

"We have not established which law the entire ascent violated. Write that forces blocking a gathering containing armed men encountered resistance."

The correction narrowed the claim and widened the danger. A gathering containing armed men could also contain people who had committed no offense.

"Include the command to allow unarmed departure."

"The first or the second?"

Pontius heard Claudia in the question.

"Both, with their times."

The initial order had allowed withdrawal. The field report showed that the road arrangement might have made withdrawal impossible when force began. His later order created routes after casualties. A Roman examiner would see the sequence.

He could combine the instructions as one continuing purpose. He chose not to alter the times. An honest record might protect him from the claim that he intended massacre.

It could not protect him from the consequences of command.

## Prisoners

The commander returned the next day. Dust covered his cloak to the knees. He requested a private military hearing before entering any count in the civil record.

Pontius refused.

"The number belongs in both."

"The count includes men found after the road cleared. We cannot say every body came from cavalry action."

"Then divide what you can establish."

The commander reported dead from sword, spear, trampling, and wounds whose causes had not been recorded before removal. The total was grave enough that Pontius made him repeat it. Many slain, many taken. No count had been made in the field by anyone without a reason to shape it, and Pontius could be no more certain than the report in front of him.

"How many bore arms?"

"Among the dead?"

"Among dead and captured."

"Weapons were collected after families and animals crossed the ground. Ownership cannot be fixed in every case."

"How many prisoners were taken holding weapons?"

The commander gave a smaller number.

"And the rest?"

"Some were pointed out as leaders. Some resisted arrest. Some were caught near men who fought."

"Near is not a charge."

"They were moving toward a prophet who promised to uncover royal treasure."

"Sacred vessels," Pontius said.

The commander hesitated. "The council notice called it treasure."

"The council notice called him a deceiver who promised sacred vessels."

"Which could fund revolt."

"Could."

Pontius ordered the unarmed prisoners reviewed individually. Those with no act beyond presence would be released after names and villages were recorded. Men identified by two witnesses as attacking soldiers would remain. Alleged leaders required a stated act or evidence of command.

The commander objected that released men would carry the story to every Samaritan town.

"Every household on that road will carry it already."

"Then we should keep those who shape it."

"On what charge?"

The commander had none that could survive a Syrian review.

Pontius dismissed him and read the casualty tablet again.

He had imagined the road as space that soldiers could open and close. The crowd had occupied it with bodies, carts, animals, fear, and unequal knowledge. A warning given at the front became no warning at the rear. A withdrawal route written in Caesarea became a cavalry edge already moving.

He would not accept that these failures made his order irrational. The gathering contained arms. Its leader promised revelation from a sacred mountain. Local councils warned of danger. If he had waited and violence followed, the same accusers would say he ignored notice.

The mistake lay in execution.

That conclusion allowed him to remain a competent prefect correcting officers.

It did not explain why he had sent cavalry before the crowd's composition could be learned.

## The Road Examined

Pontius went north to the road on the third day.

His advisers opposed the journey. A prefect walking ground after an action could suggest that the first military account had failed. Remaining in Caesarea would let hostile witnesses claim he never looked at what he ordered.

He chose a small escort and left the standards behind.

The village below the ascent smelled of opened earth and vinegar used to wash wounds. Carts had cut parallel ruts until the place where cavalry crossed them. There the tracks broke into churned ground. Sandals, broken baskets, a child's wooden cup, and two sling stones remained among the hoof marks.

The commander pointed toward the front of the former gathering.

"The armed group held here."

"How do you know?"

"Weapons were recovered along this line."

"After people ran across it."

"Yes."

The interpreter had stood on a low wall to give the warning. Pontius climbed to the same place. From there he could see the road turn behind an olive stand. Anyone beyond the turn could not have seen the cavalry front. Wind carried his voice toward the mountain and away from people approaching behind him.

"How many times did you call?"

"Three."

"In which languages?"

"Greek, then the local speech through my interpreter."

"At each call?"

"The first in both. Then the press increased."

The written summary said warnings had been repeated. It did not say the repeated warnings reached fewer people in fewer tongues.

A Samaritan elder brought two survivors under safe conduct. One had a bandaged scalp and said he came because his sister believed the vessels would prove an ancient promise. He carried a staff. The other had joined cousins who expected treasure and admitted that two cousins bore swords.

"Did the prophet call you to fight Rome?" Pontius asked.

The first said no.

The second said he had never heard the prophet directly.

"Who threw at the horse?"

Neither knew.

"Did men resist the cavalry?"

"After the horses entered," the first said.

"Before," the commander said.

Pontius asked each where he had stood. Their positions did not overlap. Both accounts could be partial and true. The first violence stayed unfixed between a missile no witness before him could trace and a trumpet call the messenger could not name.

At the edge of the ground, physicians worked beneath an awning. Pontius had ordered treatment without inquiry. A civil clerk had begun writing names anyway, saying families needed a record to recover the wounded.

"Who sees this list?" Pontius asked.

"The medical officer, the review panel, and the village representative."

"No military recruiter. No tax collector. Seal a copy for the families."

The order prevented one abuse and could not make the names safe from every later use.

He spoke with a cavalryman whose horse had fallen. The man swore a stone struck first. He had seen it leave the crowd and could identify no hand. He believed armed men meant to break through.

"Believed when?"

"When the stone struck."

"Before the trumpet?"

"I heard the trumpet as my horse rose."

The sequence narrowed and refused to settle.

Pontius walked the withdrawal edge opened after the charge. It crossed a drainage cut too steep for carts. Soldiers had lifted children over it while mounted men drove others away from the road. One act could be rescue from a danger the same formation had enlarged.

On the return journey, the decurion rode beside him.

"Would you give the same order now?" the old officer asked.

Pontius disliked questions from subordinates shaped as counsel.

"I would hold the road earlier."

"With cavalry?"

"With enough infantry to separate the approaches before the body grew."

"That would require acting on the first notice."

"Yes."

The lesson he carried from the examined ground was still speed. He changed the composition of force and the arrangement of routes. He did not abandon the belief that uncertainty became safer when Rome reached it first.

## Claudia's Copy

Claudia requested the prisoner lists for the relief account.

Pontius denied the full list and authorized names of injured noncombatants after release. She could pay households without gaining access to men still under inquiry.

"How will the clerk decide who is a noncombatant?" she asked.

"By the review I ordered."

"The same officers seized them."

"A civil scribe will sit with the panel."

"And the first report?"

"What about it?"

"It says the number and purpose of armed men were unconfirmed."

"The second report found arms."

"Among a larger body."

He felt the old tribunal pressure return: each distinction true, none of them able to decide anything.

"I ordered the road held and withdrawal allowed. The commander faced resistance."

"Was withdrawal open when he advanced?"

"That is under review."

"You sent cavalry into uncertainty."

"I sent cavalry to prevent uncertainty from occupying a mountain."

She held his gaze. "That is what changed."

"Since when?"

"Since five days taught you that waiting lets people acquire power."

The accuracy angered him because it made prudence sound like fear.

"Five days taught me that a governor who permits a challenge to assemble will be judged by whatever it becomes."

"And this?"

"Will be judged by what occurred, not by the danger you prefer to imagine absent."

"I asked you to learn who was there."

"I knew enough to act."

He ended the audience.

Later he asked the legal scribe whether Claudia possessed a copy of the first station report. The man said Marcus had copied arrival language into the household messenger register before the military summary was written. The copy was permitted under existing household access.

Pontius could order it surrendered. Doing so would create a second fact more dangerous than the first: the prefect altered access after a bloody action.

"Leave it," he said.

He told himself the exact wording supported him. Armed men had been reported. Delay threatened the pass. The order distinguished those who could withdraw.

Exact words had never prevented an examiner from arranging them against their author.

The prisoner review lasted into the fourth day. Forty-one men were released for lack of an act that could be stated. Others remained for assault, possession of weapons after warning, or alleged leadership. Pontius required the last category reviewed again. Three accusations came from the same village rival and collapsed when the civil scribe separated the witnesses.

The releases angered the commander, who said Rome was returning organizers to the council preparing complaint.

"If they organized an attack, prove it," Pontius said.

"If we wait for proof, we learn after revolt."

Pontius heard his own governing fear spoken back to him without refinement.

"A prefect may act against danger. A jailer still needs a charge."

He released the three.

The distinction mattered to those men. It did not restore the dead or remove the petition already forming.

## The Council's Version

Samaritan elders arrived under civic seal before Pontius completed the prisoner reviews.

They did not request a public audience. They asked to submit names of the dead, recover bodies, and challenge the detention of men they described as refugees from cavalry. Their spokesman did not defend the prophet's promise. He called the man's claim foolish and Rome's answer murderous.

"Your own council warned this office," Pontius said.

"A council warned that people were gathering. It did not ask you to cut them down."

"Armed men resisted."

"Then name them."

Pontius had names for prisoners captured with weapons. He had incomplete ownership for weapons collected from the ground. He had no clean division among the dead.

"A review is in progress."

"While families bury what your horses left."

The interpreter softened the sentence in Greek. Pontius asked for the original and made him repeat it.

"The cavalry acted after warning."

"Some heard a warning. Some heard shouting. Some saw people ahead turn and soldiers enter them."

"Were you present?"

"No. My sister's sons were."

"Did they carry arms?"

"One carried a road knife. One carried his child."

Pontius could not verify either claim in the room.

He offered release of bodies without fee, medical access, and prompt individual review. The spokesman accepted the bodies and rejected the adequacy of the rest.

"We will petition the legate."

There it was: the road to Syria spoken without disguise.

Pontius kept his voice even. "You may petition any authority allowed by law. This office will send its record."

The elders withdrew.

He immediately ordered the completed field messages, warnings, prisoner reviews, casualty divisions, and relief instructions copied for Vitellius. The file would show notice of a dangerous gathering, weapons, resistance, measures to allow withdrawal, later restraint, and lawful review.

It would also show Claudia's requested verification arrived before contact and had not delayed his order.

He considered omitting a private household message from the official packet. It was not military advice. It held no office.

The legal scribe said, "The station report she cited is already in the messenger register."

Omitting her request would make the surviving sequence look cleaner until someone asked why the household had copied the qualification.

"Include it as an unsolicited household communication," Pontius said.

The phrase reduced its authority without concealing its existence.

He signed the cover letter before sunset.

The Samaritan elders did not wait for his packet. Their delegation left by the inland road with names, family accounts, and the authority of a council that could say it had tried the prefect first.

Pontius had acted before a gathering could become a complaint.

The complaint was already riding toward Vitellius.
