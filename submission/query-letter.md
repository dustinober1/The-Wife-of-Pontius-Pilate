Claudia was raised in another man's house and taught, before she was ten, that men put respectable reasons in letters and the true reason enters by another door. It made her useful. It got her married to an ambitious equestrian officer with a posting no one else wanted. It did not prepare her for how quickly a province can kill someone while every official involved keeps his hands clean.

THE WIFE OF PONTIUS PILATE is an 81,000-word work of historical fiction.

Judaea, A.D. 26. Claudia arrives with her husband and negotiates the only authority available to a Roman wife: the household accounts, the guest lists, the schedule of delegations. It is enough to notice that her husband's predecessor recorded three religious complaints as trivial and paid for extra patrols in the same week. Over ten years she builds a practice out of that discrepancy, comparing Roman dispatches against Temple notices, Herodian courtesies, and what the woman who buys the fish heard at market. She keeps a casualty ledger the army does not. She learns to record who could have known a thing, and how.

She is not a spy. Her secretary Marcus is a literate enslaved man whose every errand risks the lash, and she never once forgets that using him is using property. Her friend Tamar is free, local, and entirely willing to tell her that a choice offered by the prefect's wife is still heard with the prefect behind it.

By the Passover of A.D. 33, Claudia can prove that the case against a Galilean teacher is being assembled backward from the words Rome punishes. She cannot prove it fast enough, to the right person, through a door that will open. Her husband, whose patron in Rome has been executed and who now governs to prevent complaints, has stopped being able to afford the truth.

Nothing here turns on a conspiracy. The catastrophe is built from procedure, jurisdiction, festival crowding, and rational self-interest, and it is the more frightening for it. Jesus never appears as a viewpoint and is never explained; Claudia sees him bound, across a courtyard, at forty yards.

The novel will appeal to readers of Hilary Mantel's WOLF HALL for its close-third political machinery, Robert Harris's IMPERIUM for Roman institutional thriller, and Colm Tóibín's THE TESTAMENT OF MARY for its restraint around sacred material.
