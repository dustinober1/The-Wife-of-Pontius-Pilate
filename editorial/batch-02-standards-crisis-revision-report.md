## Final chapter verdicts

### Chapter 5: The Standards at Night — ACCEPTED

The chapter remains in Claudia's close-third POV. The standards enter Jerusalem covered at night and are raised at the Antonia. Roman command, gate, liaison, household, service-market, and military-schedule records produce partial and conflicting accounts. Claudia delays the first Syrian packet, puts the liaison household witness and later formal protest into Pontius's information, opens a guarded service-market route at personal cost, protects Tamar's leave, and compares official head counts with receipts and local arrivals. She identifies the stadium convergence through schedules and armory issues, decides which facts to tell Pontius, protects Tamar's name, and changes the material he carries into the sixth day. Pontius retains the stadium order.

The protest forms in Caesarea and endures through five days. Exact crowd totals remain unfixed. Tamar knows Yaakov and family preparations but cannot speak for the gathering. Marcus knows schedules, messenger order, and record movement but has no command access. Philotas knows household procedure. Officers know assigned ground. The liaison knows priestly-household sequence. Each source remains bounded.

### Chapter 6: What the Crowd Taught Him — ACCEPTED

The chapter remains entirely in Pontius POV. His objective is to end the protest without surrendering authority, losing troop control, or producing a fatal complaint. The stadium plan is rational: controlled entrances, separated tiers, a public offer, an open exit, and force displayed under two signals. The plan fails when the protesters, with varied motives and visible fear, accept its stated cost and bare their necks.

Pontius alone lowers his hand, orders the swords sheathed, dictates removal of the images, and writes the retreat as his own judgment. He weighs crowd discipline, soldier reliability, riot risk, competing witnesses, Syrian and Roman complaint, festival governability, and the limits of patronage. The retreat prevents massacre and humiliates him publicly. His wrong lesson becomes command practice: shorten the interval, act before opposition forms, conceal force until it can close, specify the unit's end before deployment, and send the first account with the order.

### Chapter 7: A Woman Named Tamar — ACCEPTED

The chapter remains in Claudia's close-third POV and preserves Tamar, Rivkah, Yaakov, mourning, Tamar's free status, and Claudia's Roman rank. Yaakov's shoulder injury links the household directly to the standards protest. He refuses palace medicine and gives a partial account of the stadium. Claudia holds Tamar's place and wages, pays added watches, secures bounded paid absence, and arranges real light work for Yaakov through Mattan.

Claudia and Marcus create a limited held-wage, burial, physician, and injury-account practice. Tamar rejects compulsory family naming and forces a safer divided record. Mattan holds consent and local identity; Marcus holds date, sum, purpose, witness, and payment movement. A Roman clerk tries to identify protest-linked families, exposing the practice's risk. Claudia restricts his access, revises the records, absorbs staff resentment and nearly half a quarterly allowance, and loses Marcus's sight of military schedules after Pontius closes that channel. Tamar's trust remains conditional. The chapter ends with Claudia responsible for protecting the people named or implied by the practice.
