# Passion Week Harmonization Control

## Status and purpose

This control records the novel’s deliberate narrative synthesis for Chapters 21–26. It does not claim that the combined sequence is historically recoverable. Gospel traditions differ in chronology, geography, participants, wording, and individual episodes. The novel preserves those limits in prose through partial witnesses, shaped reports, and Claudia’s restricted viewpoint.

## Locked book chronology

- The novel uses Passover A.D. 33 as its controlled dramatic choice. A.D. 30 remains a major historical alternative.
- The prefectural household relocates from Caesarea to Jerusalem before the feast for public-order administration.
- Jesus’s entry, Temple disturbance, arrest, hearings, Roman presentation, execution, burial, and empty-tomb reports occur in that order.

## Entry and Temple action

- Claudia receives the entry and Temple-action events through Roman, Temple, Herodian, and household reports rather than omniscient narration.
- Crowd size remains unnumbered or operationally described.
- Royal acclamation, Temple authority dispute, and disruption of trade or exchange remain distinct report components.
- No report establishes Jesus’s private intent.

## Arrest and local hearings

- The adopted synthesis places the arrest at night.
- The prisoner is routed first through a preliminary inquiry associated with Annas’s family household, then through Caiaphas’s serving high-priestly office and a council consultation before Roman presentation.
- This route combines Johannine and Synoptic traditions as a declared narrative choice.
- Claudia cannot observe closed hearings. She reconstructs order from messengers, custody marks, schedules, servants, and partial statements.
- Annas remains the former high priest and family elder; Caiaphas remains the serving high priest.

## Roman presentation and tribunal geography

- The Roman hearing occurs in a secured paved court associated in the household with Herod’s palace, where Pontius stays during the feast.
- Some characters call the pavement the Lithostrotos; the narration states that ancient geography and later identification are disputed.
- The bema is a movable or locally installed Roman judgment seat used in the chosen court. The novel does not claim a settled archaeological location for Gospel Gabbatha.
- Claudia observes from palace rooms and terrace access already established in the Prologue. She never enters the tribunal as a magistrate.

## Charge translation

- Local religious allegations concern authority, sacred law, Temple conduct, and claims judged internally.
- The Roman presentation emphasizes misleading the people, tribute, disturbance, and kingship.
- Witnesses and summaries remain inconsistent. Pontius recognizes that evidence for violent sedition is weak.
- Caiaphas may shape institutional presentation, but no single mastermind controls every clerk, crowd member, official, or outcome.

## Antipas referral

- The novel includes Luke’s Antipas referral as a narrative choice.
- It occurs after initial Roman questioning identifies Galilean jurisdiction and before final Roman disposition.
- Antipas declines responsibility and returns Jesus without resolving the charge.
- No Antipas POV or private motive is narrated. The returned custody paper and messenger route show the institutional effect.

## Barabbas and release custom

- The novel includes the Gospel Barabbas episode while stating through narration or report limits that no independent surviving rule establishes a regular Passover release custom.
- Pontius uses an available or asserted festival clemency practice as a political maneuver, not a neutral election.
- The gathered crowd is the court crowd, accusers, attendants, petitioners, and people drawn to the hearing. It is never equated with all Jerusalem or all Jewish people.
- Barabbas is associated in the adopted tradition with violent unrest; exact acts and the shape of his following remain limited to the Roman custody record used in scene.

## Scourging and public presentation

- The novel includes scourging and public presentation as an attempted coercive compromise before final sentence.
- The violence is neither salvific spectacle nor proof of Pontius’s reluctance. It is a Roman act ordered within his effort to avoid a politically costly acquittal or execution.

## Claudia’s warning

- The Prologue’s note wording, Marcus route, legal-scribe delivery, household seal, and public receipt remain unchanged.
- Chapters 21–23 may supply earlier attempts to secure written charges or delay, but cannot duplicate or invalidate the Prologue’s final note.
- The dream motivates urgency and moral language but does not prove innocence. Claudia’s evidence comes from source comparison, weak sedition proof, shaped translation, custody irregularity, and Pontius’s available options.

## Handwashing

- The novel includes Matthew’s handwashing tradition as a public disclaimer chosen by Pontius after he orders or authorizes the sentence.
- The act does not transfer legal responsibility, erase Roman command, or assign collective guilt.
- Claudia interprets only what she can observe: Pontius attempts to separate his public image from his decision.

## Sentence and responsibility

- Pontius alone makes the Roman decision authorizing crucifixion.
- Priesthood pressure, court-crowd pressure, complaint risk, Caesar-loyalty language, Antipas’s return, and procedural momentum narrow his options without removing agency.
- Jesus receives no POV, private motive, devotional identity declaration, or omniscient moral commentary.
- Collective blame against Jewish people is prohibited. Institutions, officials, witnesses, attendants, petitioners, soldiers, and bystanders remain differentiated.
