# Questions for Discussion

1. Claudia's guardian tells her, "Men put respectable reasons in letters. The true reason often enters by another door." How does this early lesson shape everything she does for the rest of the novel, for better and for worse?

2. Claudia never holds office, commands nothing, and cannot enter most of the rooms where decisions are made. What kinds of power does she have instead? Where do you see those limits protect her, and where do you see them fail the people she is trying to help?

3. Marcus is indispensable to Claudia's work and enslaved for nearly all of the book. Discuss the scene in which Claudia sets aside money to purchase his freedom. Does it change how you read everything that came before it?

4. Tamar repeatedly corrects Claudia, sometimes sharply: "That is permission to lie for you." "A choice offered by the prefect's wife is still heard with the prefect behind it." Why does the novel need Tamar to push back this hard? What would the book lose if Tamar simply trusted Claudia?

5. Pontius Pilate is given a stated creed: "an even hand and an unbending spine." Trace how that principle curdles across the book, from the standards crisis through Gerizim. At what point, if any, do you think he could still have chosen differently?

6. The standards crisis ends with Pontius avoiding a massacre and immediately drawing the wrong lesson from it. Why does the novel treat that near miss as the origin of the tragedy rather than its opposite?

7. After the aqueduct riot, Sejanus's letter calls the violence "competence" and Pontius accepts an absolution he never asked for. What does this scene suggest about how institutions launder violence into administration?

8. John the Baptist's death reaches Claudia through four incompatible accounts that are never reconciled. What does the novel gain, dramatically and morally, by refusing to tell you what really happened?

9. Jesus never becomes a viewpoint character and is seen only from a distance, through reports that disagree with one another. What was it like to read a novel about the days leading to the crucifixion in which the central figure remains, by design, unknowable?

10. Claudia's warning to Pontius, the single line the historical record actually gives her, arrives before judgment and changes nothing. Why do you think the author chose to have her intervention fail exactly where the Gospel account says it failed?

11. The novel insists there is no single conspiracy, only interacting institutions and people behaving rationally in their own interest. Which of those institutions, Roman, Temple, Herodian, do you think bears the most responsibility? Does the question even hold together, given what the book shows you?

12. Claudia keeps a private ledger of the dead that the official Roman account omits. What does the act of record-keeping mean in this novel? Who is allowed to write history, and who is only allowed to remember it?

13. By the end, Claudia tells Pontius she will not decide whether to leave him while his case is unfinished, because she will not let her departure be read as a verdict Rome denied him. What did you make of that ending? Is it a form of loyalty, a form of self-protection, or something else?

14. Tiberius dies while Pontius is still at sea, so the hearing he was recalled to face never happens. The novel then declines to invent what became of any of its characters. How did that choice sit with you, coming at the end of a story built so carefully out of evidence and its absence?

15. If you know the Gospel accounts, what surprised you most about seeing this story from the household of the man who signed the sentence?
